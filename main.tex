%% ----- Abstract ------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
\noindent Whether people who receive help online go on to help others is an open question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and designs that ignore prior user activity may overstate reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions were answered with propensity-score-matched users whose questions were not. Rather than asking whether reciprocity works in general, we locate the narrow conditions under which it moves behavior. Users whose questions were answered helped others slightly more often (increment at answer arrival, hazard ratio [HR]\,$=$\,1.049, 95\% CI [1.044, 1.054]). The response depended on tenure: newcomers responded most (HR\,$=$\,1.071 in the first week, 95\% CI [1.056, 1.086]), while the longest-tenured users did not respond measurably. The tenure gradient is the most robust pattern here. Timing mattered too, though far more fragilely: users answered within 30 to 60 minutes responded most strongly, while answers under 30 minutes show no elevation. Most of our data predate 2020, and the newcomer response does not survive a split at ChatGPT's release, so we read these estimates as a pre-generative-AI baseline rather than a general test of reciprocity. Gratitude, moral obligation, social expectation, and incentive displacement remain proposed mechanisms rather than measured ones. On this one platform, generalized reciprocity works less as a general engine of cooperation than as a contributor-recruitment channel at the point of entry: designers gain most by getting newcomers' questions answered.
\end{abstract}
