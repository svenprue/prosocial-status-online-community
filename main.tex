%% ----- Abstract ------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
\noindent Generalized reciprocity is an often-cited principle in human cooperation, but whether people who receive help online subsequently help others remains an open empirical question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and popular empirical designs ignore the temporal order of helping and being helped, potentially mistaking general user activity for reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions were answered with propensity-score-matched users whose questions were not. Overall, we find that reciprocity plays a fairly modest role on Stack Overflow. We identify the narrow conditions under which it occurs at all. Users whose questions were answered helped others slightly more often (hazard ratio [HR]\,$=$\,1.049, 95\% CI [1.044, 1.054]). The response is concentrated among newcomers (HR\,$=$\,1.071 in the first week, 95\% CI [1.056, 1.086]), while the longest-tenured users did not respond measurably. That tenure moderates reciprocity is the most robust pattern we find. Timing mattered too: users who receive an answer within 30 to 60 minutes responded most strongly, while answers under 30 minutes show no elevation. Most of our data predate 2020, and the reciprocity effect among newcomers disappears after the release of ChatGPT. We discuss, but cannot directly measure, different potential mechanisms of gratitude, moral obligation, social expectation, and incentive displacement. On the platform we study, generalized reciprocity works less as a general catalyst of cooperation than as a contributor-recruitment channel at the point of entry: designers gain most by getting newcomers' questions answered.
\end{abstract}
