\begin{abstract}
\noindent Whether receiving help on an online platform increases subsequent helping is an open empirical question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and designs that do not net out baseline user activity may overstate reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions received an answer with propensity-score-matched users whose questions did not. Receiving an answer is associated with a small increase in subsequent helping (pooled summed DiD HR\,$=$\,1.03), but the association is steeply graded by tenure: it is largest among newcomers (HR\,$=$\,1.13 in the first week) and undetectable among users with more than three years of experience. The newcomer gradient is the most robust pattern in the data --- it holds on the pre-2020, pre-generative-AI subsample --- yet the pooled effect is modest in absolute terms and reverses once we control for the quality of the answer received, and answer speed moderates it weakly and inconsistently. The data identify short-run behavioral associations, not the psychological mechanisms (gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement) that theory proposes. These findings provide a better-identified short-run behavioral test of generalized reciprocity in one large technical Q\&A community and suggest tentative, newcomer-focused design implications.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} generalized reciprocity, online communities, knowledge sharing, survival analysis, platform design, Stack Overflow
\end{abstract}
