%% ----- Abstract ------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
\noindent Whether people who receive help on an online platform go on to help others is an open empirical question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and designs that do not account for how active a user already was may overstate reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions received an answer with propensity-score-matched users whose questions did not. Users whose questions were answered went on to answer other people's questions slightly more often (increment at answer arrival, hazard ratio [HR]\,$=$\,1.049, 95\% CI [1.044, 1.054]). The response depended on tenure: newcomers responded most (HR\,$=$\,1.071 in the first week, 95\% CI [1.056, 1.086]), while the longest-tenured users did not respond measurably at all. This newcomer pattern is the most robust in the data. Timing also mattered: Faster answers led to more help, but users who answered within 30 to 60 minutes responded most strongly, while the fastest answers, arriving in under 30 minutes, show no elevation. We explore different explanations and implications of this pattern. Our contribution is behavioral: we identify the short-run consequence that theory predicts, while gratitude, moral obligation, social expectation, and incentive displacement remain proposed mechanisms rather than measured ones. The pattern is consistent with generalized reciprocity working less as a general engine of cooperation than as a contributor-recruitment channel at the point of entry: designers gain most by making sure newcomers' questions get answers.
\end{abstract}
