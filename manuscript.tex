\title{At the Point of Entry: Receiving Help and Newcomer Contribution on Stack Overflow}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Whether people who receive help on an online platform go on to help others is an open empirical question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and designs that do not account for how active a user already was may overstate reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions received an answer with propensity-score-matched users whose questions did not. Users whose questions were answered went on to answer other people's questions slightly more often (increment at answer arrival, hazard ratio [HR]\,$=$\,1.049, 95\% CI [1.044, 1.054]). The response depended on tenure: newcomers responded most (HR\,$=$\,1.07 in the first week), while the longest-tenured users did not respond measurably at all. This newcomer pattern is the most robust in the data and is present in the pre-2020 period, before generative-AI tools arrived. Timing also mattered: users answered within 30 to 60 minutes responded most strongly, while the very fastest answers, arriving in under 30 minutes, show no elevation. We read this as qualified support for an immediacy account, though we cannot fully separate how quickly an answer arrived from how good it was. Our data identify short-run behavioral patterns, not the psychological mechanisms (gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement) that theory proposes. The pattern is consistent with generalized reciprocity working less as a general engine of cooperation than as a contributor-recruitment channel at the point of entry: designers gain most by making sure newcomers' questions get answers.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Ethics statement}
This study analyzed de-identified, publicly available archival records from Stack Overflow. No interaction with human participants occurred, and no personally identifiable information was collected or analyzed. Ethical approval was not required under applicable institutional policies for secondary analysis of public platform data.

\section*{Data availability}
Stack Overflow public data dumps were obtained from the Internet Archive (\url{https://archive.org/details/stackexchange}). Analysis code and step-by-step instructions to reproduce all tables and figures are available in a public repository (URL withheld for blind review). Processed analytical datasets used in the matching and survival analyses are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.
