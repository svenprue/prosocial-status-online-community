\title{Receiving Help and Short-Run Contribution on Stack Overflow: A Newcomer-Concentrated Association}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Whether receiving help on an online platform increases subsequent helping is an open empirical question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and designs that do not net out baseline user activity may overstate reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions received an answer with propensity-score-matched users whose questions did not. Once self-directed accept events are excluded from the helping outcome, the pooled post-answer elevation is indistinguishable from null (summed DiD HR\,$=$\,0.998). A modest association is concentrated among newcomers (HR\,$=$\,1.11 in the first week of tenure) and absent among the most experienced users (HR\,$=$\,0.995). Answer speed moderates the effect weakly and inconsistently. The data identify short-run behavioral associations, not the psychological mechanisms (gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement) that theory proposes. These findings provide a better-identified short-run behavioral test of generalized reciprocity in one large technical Q\&A community and suggest tentative, newcomer-focused design implications.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Ethics statement}
This study analyzed de-identified, publicly available archival records from Stack Overflow. No interaction with human participants occurred, and no personally identifiable information was collected or analyzed. Ethical approval was not required under applicable institutional policies for secondary analysis of public platform data.

\section*{Data availability}
Stack Overflow public data dumps were obtained from the Internet Archive (\url{https://archive.org/details/stackexchange}). Analysis code and step-by-step instructions to reproduce all tables and figures are available in a public repository (URL withheld for blind review). Processed analytical datasets used in the matching and survival analyses are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.
