\section{Robustness: Cohort Heterogeneity, Observable Selection, and Answer Quality}
\label{sec:robustness_revision}

Table~\ref{tab:cohort_robustness} addresses the concern that pooling entry cohorts from 2008 through 2025 conflates structurally different eras of the platform (reputation inflation, tag evolution, and the post-2022 generative-AI disruption). Because calendar year enters the matching design only as an exact stratum, treated and control questions within a pair always share a year; to probe heterogeneity \emph{across} years we re-estimate the pooled Model~A on the pre-2020 subsample. The treatment effect is essentially unchanged (HR\,$=$\,1.02, versus 1.03 in the full sample), indicating that the association is not an artifact of recent cohorts and was already present before the generative-AI era.

Table~\ref{tab:newcomer_robustness} reports the newcomer ($<$\,1 week) bucket under three specifications: the baseline (HR\,$=$\,1.14), a specification adding observable pre-treatment selection controls (HR\,$=$\,1.23), and one adding answer-quality controls (HR\,$=$\,0.91). The newcomer effect is stable to --- indeed slightly amplified by --- observable-selection controls, but, like the pooled effect, reverses under answer-quality controls, reinforcing that answer quality is the most consequential confound the design does not resolve.

\section{Analysis of Life-Time Contributions}
\label{sec:lt-contributions}
What remains unclear is whether users who become contributors through generalized reciprocity continue to contribute consistently and become valuable community members. To explore this, we compare the contributions of reciprocity-activated users with those of all contributors selected for Study 1. Specifically, we examine the number of times users from each group provided and received help up to July 14, 2024. The results are presented in Table \ref{tab:quartile_statistics}. Across all quartiles and at the median, reciprocity-activated users ask and provide more help than their counterparts.
For example, at the median, reciprocity-activated users provide eight answers and ask 13 questions, compared to six answers and 11 questions among all contributors. Similarly, the 75th percentile of reciprocity-activated users provides 23 answers and asks 29 questions, compared to 19 answers and 22 questions for all contributors.
These findings suggest that users who begin contributing after receiving help become equally, if not more, engaged in the community's helping dynamics compared to all contributors. Furthermore, this analysis demonstrates that once users start contributing, the act of giving and receiving help becomes (at least temporarily) decoupled.

\section{Implementation Details} All models are estimated using the \texttt{lifelines} CoxTimeVaryingFitter with a small ridge (L2) penalizer applied for numerical stability. The penalizer strength is $\lambda = 5 \times 10^{-3}$ for main-effect models and $\lambda = 10^{-2}$ for models that include response-time interaction terms (where the interaction covariates are correlated with the base treatment indicator). These values are sufficiently small that they introduce negligible shrinkage relative to the scale of the estimated effects, but they ensure stable Hessian inversion in large risk sets. All covariates are mean-centered before fitting; response-time interaction terms are additionally clipped to their 5th--95th percentile range and z-scored as described above. The standard errors in all tables are model-based (inverse-Hessian) and are \emph{not} corrected for the dependence induced by the matched design --- repeated observations per user, reused control questions, and multiple event intervals per timeline. Because that dependence typically makes model-based standard errors too small, the appropriate correction is a cluster-robust (sandwich) variance clustered by matched pair, or a bootstrap that resamples whole matched pairs with replacement; we report matched-pair bootstrap intervals where available and expect them to be somewhat wider than the model-based intervals shown. Ridge penalization and the winsorizing and standardization of the interaction covariates additionally regularize the reported standard errors. For the pooled experienced-user models (H1), estimation was performed on a random sample of approximately 8,001,931 user-interval rows drawn from matched pairs (preserving pair integrity) to keep computation feasible; tenure-bucket-specific models (H2, H3) use all available intervals within each stratum.
