\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Whether people who receive help on an online platform go on to help others has been a persistent question in the study of online communities. Generalized reciprocity, the tendency to help others after being helped oneself \citep{nowak_upstream_2007, baker_paying_2014}, is among the mechanisms proposed to sustain cooperation on platforms like Stack Overflow and Wikipedia, where the same two people rarely trade help directly. If people who receive help reliably go on to give it, cooperation may sustain itself through cascading helping acts, even among strangers \citep{fowler_cooperative_2010}. The behavioral evidence, however, is inconsistent: some observational studies find substantial reciprocity effects \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_why_2019}, while others find weak or null relationships \citep{wasko_why_2005, joyce_predicting_2006}. We argue that much of this inconsistency is methodological. Our contribution is a cleaner short-run test on one platform: whether users help more after someone answers their own question. While data do not directly measure the psychological mechanisms that theory proposes (gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement), we uniquely test the behavioral consequence those mechanisms predict.

Researchers who study reciprocity on platforms face a persistent confound: more active users are generally more likely to post questions or answers. Studies that correlate help received with help given over an aggregate period of time, without accounting for how active a user already was, conflate reciprocity with general engagement \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_engaging_2018, joyce_predicting_2006}. Prior designs have also largely treated receiving help as a one-time event, even though people ask for and give help continuously.

We address both problems by developing a matched difference-in-differences (DiD) survival analysis, applied to over 21 million questions from Stack Overflow. For each question a user posts, we define observation windows before and after the question and compare changes in helping behavior between users whose question received an answer (treated) and propensity-score-matched users whose question did not (control). We use Cox proportional hazards models, which accommodate the continuous timing of helping and the staggered arrival of answers across users.

We develop two theoretical predictions alongside the basic reciprocity hypothesis (H1). Drawing on theories of socialization and norm internalization, we predict that the reciprocity effect is stronger among newcomers than among experienced users (H2). Newcomers have not yet internalized community-specific norms or learned to value platform incentives such as reputation points; general moral obligations to reciprocate are therefore more likely to drive their behavior. As users accumulate experience, these general-purpose triggers may be displaced by community-specific motivations. We also predict that faster responses amplify the reciprocity effect (H3), because gratitude is more intense when help arrives while the need is still salient \citep{mccullough_is_2001, tsang_gratitude_2006}.

The results support the baseline reciprocity hypothesis (H1) in a modest form, and offer support for the tenure gradient (H2) and the immediacy pattern (H3). Compared with their matched controls and with their own pre-question baseline, users who receive an answer go on to answer other people's questions more often: the pooled increment at answer arrival is HR\,$=$\,1.049 (95\% CI [1.044, 1.054], $p<.001$). How strongly users respond depends on how long they have been on the platform: newcomers respond most (HR\,$=$\,1.07 in the first week, $p < .001$), and the longest-tenured users do not respond measurably at all. Throughout, we estimate the increment at answer arrival --- the difference-in-differences contrast that removes any treated--control divergence arising before the answer. We report the waiting-period term separately, as a parallel-trends check rather than part of the effect, and we re-estimate the model under broader outcome definitions that add lower-cost contributions, namely comments and edits (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}).

Our study makes three contributions. First, using a design built to remove the activity confound that inflates observational estimates, we provide a cleaner short-run test: users who receive an answer help more afterward (HR\,$=$\,1.05), but by much less than prior observational studies report. Second, reciprocity is strongest among newcomers and veterans show none --- the tenure gradient is the most robust and informative pattern in the data. Third, we uncover an inverted U-shaped relationship where moderate response time leads to the biggest effect. We discuss implications for platform designers and future platform research in the age of GenAI.

\section{Theory}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generalized Reciprocity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A central mechanism proposed to support cooperation in online communities is \textit{generalized reciprocity}, the tendency for individuals who have received help to help others in turn, even when the recipient is not the original benefactor \citep{nowak_upstream_2007, fowler_cooperative_2010}. Unlike direct reciprocity, it requires no repeated exchange with the same person: it operates as a pay-it-forward principle, sustaining collective contributions in groups --- such as online knowledge-sharing platforms --- where the same two people rarely interact twice.

Socio-psychological research identifies two complementary mechanisms underlying generalized reciprocity: moral reasoning and emotional response. On the moral side, individuals reason by universalization, asking what would happen if all members requested more help than they gave. Widespread free-riding would undermine the community as a source of help, and this conclusion generates a shared obligation to reciprocate toward the group \citep{levine_logic_2020, curry_is_2019}. On the emotional side, gratitude aroused by the perceived generosity of others motivates prosocial action beyond direct reciprocation \citep{mccullough_is_2001, tsang_gratitude_2006, bartlett_gratitude_2006} and is especially relevant where free riding is easy, as on knowledge-sharing platforms \citep{molm_building_2007, elster_norms_2009}. These moral and emotional pathways are the \textit{proposed} generative mechanisms behind generalized reciprocity.

Evidence that generalized reciprocity can operate and can initiate cascading acts of generosity \citep{fowler_cooperative_2010, boyd_evolution_1989} is robust: comparative research indicates that the underlying norm is globally shared \citep{curry_is_2019}, and experimental demonstrations span laboratory \citep{greiner_indirect_2005, molm_building_2007, stanca_measuring_2009} and field settings \citep{mujcic_indirect_2018, fowler_cooperative_2010}. The open question is how strongly it operates among real users on large platforms --- a question observational data have answered inconsistently, for reasons we argue are largely methodological.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Empirical Challenges in Measuring Generalized Reciprocity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

One challenge is that much of the evidence relies on what contributors say about their own motives, in surveys and interviews. Contributors frequently describe reciprocity as a central reason for their engagement \citep{wasko_it_2000, lakhani_how_2003}. Yet people have difficulty articulating the moral motives behind their actions \citep{vaisey_motivation_2009, mcclelland_how_1989}, and introspection struggles to separate reciprocity from altruism, conformity to perceived norms, status pursuit, or the popular rhetoric of ``giving back'' as post-hoc rationalization \citep{batson1991altruism, benabou_incentives_2006}. Self-reported data may therefore overstate the prevalence of reciprocity as a discrete motive.

The deeper challenge is that the people who ask for help are also the people most likely to give it. Users who ask questions on platforms like Stack Overflow answer them at disproportionately high rates --- not because receiving help triggered a reciprocal response, but because both behaviors reflect the same underlying characteristics, such as greater platform engagement. Studies that correlate help received with help given without accounting for this baseline activity effectively confound reciprocity with user type \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_engaging_2018, joyce_predicting_2006}. This confound is the central identification challenge that motivates the matched difference-in-differences design we develop in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

Prior observational studies of online reciprocity report mixed magnitudes. \citet{baker_paying_2014} and \citet{chen_why_2019} find positive associations between help received and subsequent helping; \citet{wasko_why_2005} and \citet{joyce_predicting_2006} find weak or null relationships. Our matched DiD estimates sit below the larger positive reports, consistent with the view that designs that do not net out baseline activity overstate the effect. The tenure gradient --- clear generalized reciprocity among newcomers, null among veterans --- offers one reconciliation of the mixed prior findings.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reciprocity and the User Trajectory}
\label{sec:trajectory}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We propose that the mixed findings in the literature can be partly reconciled by attending to a dimension that prior research has not received proper attention: the user trajectory from newcomer to experienced contributor. We argue that generalized reciprocity is strongest for new users and attenuates as users gain experience on the platform.

The logic of this prediction draws on theories of socialization and norm internalization. When a user first encounters an online community, they are unfamiliar with its specific norms, social structures, and reward systems. At this stage, community-specific incentives, such as reputation points, badges, or leaderboard positions, carry little motivational weight because the newcomer has not yet learned to value them. Instead, initial contributions are guided by more general moral principles that operate independently of community-specific knowledge. Generalized reciprocity is precisely such a principle: it requires only that the individual has received help and feels a moral obligation or emotional impulse to help in return, without any need to understand or identify with the particular community \citep{levine_logic_2020, curry_is_2019}.

As users interact with the community, they gradually learn its norms and develop a sense of membership. On platforms like Stack Overflow, these norms are codified in explicit reputation systems that reward helpful behavior with visible status markers. Once users internalize these community-specific norms, the broad moral obligation of reciprocity may be displaced by more localized incentive structures. The logic of universalization (``I should help because I was helped'') gives way to the logic of community membership (``I should help because that is what valued members do''). This norm displacement echoes accounts of how external incentives can crowd out moral motives \citep{benabou_incentives_2006}.

Two further mechanisms accelerate this decline. As users accumulate experiences of receiving help, each additional instance becomes less surprising, and the gratitude that fuels reciprocity fades with repetition \citep{mccullough_adaptation_2008}. And as users learn that the platform rewards helping with reputation and status, they may increasingly attribute others' helpfulness to status pursuit rather than genuine generosity, further dampening that gratitude.

This account predicts that generalized reciprocity is strongest among newcomers and declines with experience. It also helps explain why prior studies have reached different conclusions about the importance of reciprocity: those that focus on or oversample experienced users may underestimate reciprocity, while those that capture newcomer behavior may find stronger effects.

\hypmark{hyp:1}
\begin{quote}
\textsc{Hypothesis 1 (H1).} \emph{Receiving help increases a user's propensity to help others (generalized reciprocity effect).}
\end{quote}

\hypmark{hyp:2}
\begin{quote}
\textsc{Hypothesis 2 (H2).} \emph{The generalized reciprocity effect is strongest among newcomers and decreases as users gain experience on the platform.}
\end{quote}

The stakes of this prediction extend beyond reconciling mixed findings. If the reciprocity association is concentrated among newcomers, generalized reciprocity would function less as a general engine of cooperation among established members than as a \textit{contributor-recruitment} channel operating at the point of entry, before community-specific incentives take hold --- a design implication we develop in Section~\ref{sec:design_implications}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Response Time and the Immediacy of Reciprocity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The emotional mechanisms underlying reciprocity suggest that the speed of help matters for its strength. Gratitude, the primary proposed emotional driver of generalized reciprocity \citep{mccullough_is_2001}, is triggered by the perception of a benefactor's generosity, and its intensity is likely to depend on the characteristics of the helping experience --- including its timeliness.

A swift response can amplify the experience of receiving help: a fast answer signals that the community is attentive and generous, and it arrives while the asker's need is still salient, when gratitude should be most intense. Conversely, when an answer arrives hours or days later, the original need may have faded or been met elsewhere, the associative link between asking and receiving weakens, and the delay itself may signal an unresponsive community. Each of these changes attenuates the emotional and moral responses that underpin reciprocation.

These considerations lead to a straightforward prediction:

\hypmark{hyp:3}
\begin{quote}
\textsc{Hypothesis 3 (H3).} \emph{Timelier responses to a user's question strengthen the generalized reciprocity effect.}
\end{quote}

This hypothesis follows from the dominant theoretical accounts of generalized reciprocity, which emphasize emotional immediacy and the salience of the helping experience. Those accounts imply that the advantage grows monotonically as help arrives sooner. Field settings, however, introduce session dynamics --- whether the asker is still online when the answer arrives --- that laboratory theory abstracts away. We therefore treat the prediction as directional, test whether it holds monotonically, and return to this qualification when presenting the results.

\section{Empirical Setting: Stack Overflow}
\label{sec:empirical_setting}
%======================================================================

Stack Overflow is well suited to examining generalized reciprocity in online knowledge sharing. The platform publishes quarterly data dumps that record what every user did and when, reaching back to its inception. From these records we can reconstruct how people helped one another over time, and in what order they received and gave help --- the sequence on which any study of generalized reciprocity depends. Stack Overflow's active question-and-answer volume has declined sharply since the release of generative AI tools in late 2022 \citep{delriochanona_large_2024, burtch_consequences_2024}; our sample spans platform inception through 1 April 2025 and therefore covers a period that mostly predates, but extends into, the steepest LLM-driven decline. That timing makes the sample a clean pre-substitution baseline: it records what a human answering ecosystem contributed to recruiting new contributors while that ecosystem was still intact, a measurement that may not be possible to make again. We treat the findings as a historical snapshot of one large, technical, relatively anonymous, status-driven platform; external validity to other communities is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

At its core, Stack Overflow is a question-and-answer platform for software developers and technologists. As of June 2025, shortly after our sample cutoff, the site had accumulated over 24 million questions and 36 million answers from more than 29 million registered users \citep{stack_exchange_sites_2025}. Its user base spans a wide range of experience levels, from learners to professional software developers \citep{stack_overflow_stack_2024}. Questions on the platform must be tightly focused on specific programming problems; broad or opinion-based inquiries are typically closed by community moderation \citep{stack_exchange_inc_how_2024}. The most frequently discussed topics, as reflected in question tags, include JavaScript, Python, Java, C\#, PHP, Android, and HTML, spanning areas from web development and mobile applications to data science and machine learning. Stack Overflow is built around several core features: tags categorize questions by topic, facilitating expertise matching; a voting system allows users to upvote or downvote questions and answers to assess quality; and reputation points are earned for valued contributions such as providing high-quality answers \citep{stack_exchange_inc_how_2024}.

For our study, the key feature is the question-and-answer interaction between users. When a user posts a question, other users may provide answers. This creates a timestamped sequence (question posted, answer received) that we exploit to measure the temporal dynamics of generalized reciprocity. On Stack Overflow, question-askers and answerers are typically different individuals, which makes the platform well suited to studying \textit{generalized} reciprocity: users who receive help from one community member may subsequently help entirely different members.

\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
%======================================================================

To test our predictions, we develop a matched staggered difference-in-differences survival analysis that exploits the order in which users ask for and give help on Stack Overflow. Our design compares changes in helping behavior (measured as the hazard of posting an answer) before and after a user receives an answer, between users who receive an answer and matched users who do not. This approach integrates two methodological traditions: the staggered difference-in-differences framework of \citet{callaway_santanna_2021}, which accommodates variation in treatment timing and treatment effect heterogeneity, and the counting-process formulation of the Cox proportional hazards model \citep{andersen_gill_1982}, which allows treatment status and other covariates to vary continuously over the observation window.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Design}
\label{sec:study_design}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For each question posted by a user, we construct a timeline centered on two key events: the posting of the question ($T_{\text{question}}$) and, for questions that receive an answer, the time at which the first answer with a non-negative vote score is posted ($T_{\text{answer}}$). The observation window opens two days before the question ($T_{\text{question}} - 2\text{D}$) and closes two days \emph{after the answer arrives} ($T_{\text{answer}} + 2\text{D}$). The window is therefore anchored on the answer rather than on the question: the pre-question and post-answer segments each have a fixed two-day duration, while the intervening waiting period runs for the question's response time. The total timeline spans $4\text{D} + \text{RT}$ (response time), so slower-answered questions have proportionally longer timelines; this ensures every treated question --- however late its answer --- receives a full two-day post-answer phase in which we can observe the reciprocity effect.

We define three phases within each observation window (see Figure~\ref{fig:study_design}). The first phase, the \textit{pre-question phase}, is from the start of the observation window to the posting of the question ($T_{\text{question}} - 2\text{D}$ to $T_{\text{question}}$). This phase captures baseline helping activity before the focal question is asked.
The second phase, the \textit{waiting period}, is from the posting of the question to the receipt of the first answer ($T_{\text{question}}$ to $T_{\text{answer}}$). During this interval, the user has asked for help but has not yet received it. 
The final \textit{post-answer phase} is from the receipt of the answer to the end of the observation window ($T_{\text{answer}}$ to $T_{\text{answer}} + 2\text{D}$), a fixed two-day span regardless of how long the answer took to arrive. This is the phase in which the reciprocity effect, if it exists, should manifest.

A key feature of our design is that the post-answer (treated) phase begins at a different elapsed time for each observation, depending on how quickly the question receives a response.

This staggered timing of the post-answer phase corresponds to the setting studied by \citet{callaway_santanna_2021}, who show that standard two-way fixed-effects estimators can be biased when treatment timing varies, and treatment effects are heterogeneous. Our approach avoids this pitfall by explicitly modeling the time-varying change in treatment status within each observation window and by comparing treated observations only to not-yet-treated or never-treated controls.

Control users (those whose questions did not receive an answer) have no natural transition between the waiting period and the post-answer phase. To maintain temporal alignment with the matched treated observation, we assign the control observation a synthetic phase transition at the same elapsed time as the treated user's response time. That is, if the treated user's answer was posted 4 hours after their question, the matched control observation's timeline is split at $T_{\text{question}} + 4\text{h}$ and its window closes at $T_{\text{question}} + 4\text{h} + 2\text{D}$, creating an aligned ``post'' phase of the same two-day duration. Because the control inherits the treated response time, matched treated and control observations share identical window boundaries and identical two-day post-answer durations, so any differences between them in the post-answer phase are not driven by differences in the length of the observation period.

We center the survival clock in our Cox models on the posting of the question: time zero corresponds to the start of the observation window ($T_{\text{question}} - 2\text{D}$), so the question itself lands at day~2 of the timeline. Because the window closes two days after the answer, its end falls at day~$4 + \text{RT}$ rather than at a fixed day~4; a question answered within the first day therefore closes near day~4, while a question answered after three days closes near day~7. We record every help event (an answer posted by the focal user) at its exact timestamp and convert it to elapsed time on this clock.

Within each timeline, we track all instances in which the user provides an answer to another user's question. These helping events constitute the outcome in our survival analysis; the decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}) also examines broader definitions that count comments and edits. Conceptually, our design asks: Does the hazard of helping increase after a user receives an answer, relative to the change in hazard for a matched user who does not receive an answer? We present descriptive evidence on the raw help rates over this timeline in Section~\ref{sec:results} (Figure~\ref{fig:help_rate_pooled}), before turning to the formal Cox model estimates.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We used the full sample of non-deleted questions from Stack Overflow's inception on 31 July 2008 through 1 April 2025. We addressed two edge cases that would render our fixed-length observation window invalid. First, for early platform questions, the pre-period could predate the platform's launch, meaning no answers were possible at that time. We removed 1,080 such cases. Second, due to the data dump's 1 April 2025 cutoff, the post-period could be prematurely truncated. We excluded 8,029 cases affected by this. We then excluded 2,671,121 questions that received an answer from the question poster. These filtering steps resulted in a pre-matching dataset of 21,033,429 questions from 4,764,048 unique users, of which 16,034,099 (76.2\%) received at least one answer from another user (treatment-eligible) and 4,999,330 (23.8\%) received no answer (control-eligible). Matching (described below) is nearest-neighbor with replacement within exact strata and requires complete matching covariates, so 12,290,847 answered and 3,345,139 unanswered questions (15,635,986 in total) were eligible. Of the 12,290,847 eligible treated questions, 12,174,880 (99.1\%) were matched to a control; the remaining 115,967 fell outside common support, and a further 3,743,252 answered questions lacked a valid matching stratum or complete covariates. This attrition is not random: the treated propensity-score density piles up near one (Figure~\ref{fig:common_support}), so the discarded questions are disproportionately those that were almost certain to be answered. The 12,174,880 matched treated questions draw on 2,481,556 distinct control questions, each reused about 4.9 times on average (up to 625 times) to fill the control slots. The matched file therefore comprises 24,349,760 question-level rows (12,174,880 matched pairs) from 3,732,706 unique users, with a 50\% treatment rate by construction. All matched rows enter the Cox analysis; the helping outcome is the posting of an answer to another user's question (answers to one's own question do not count), yielding 3,435,973 helping events in the pooled analysis sample (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_experienced}).

\paragraph{Outcome.} The event of interest is posting an answer to another user's question. Within each window we track when (and whether) the focal user answers any other user's question. The Cox model then estimates the instantaneous hazard of this helping event as a function of time-varying covariates that capture the phase of the observation timeline and the treatment status. Answering one's own question, and self-directed acts such as accepting an answer, do not count as helping events. To address the concern that answers alone may understate lower-cost reciprocity, we re-estimate the pooled model under broader outcome definitions (comments only, edits only, and summing all these channels together) and report the full decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}.

We treat the posting of an answer as the theoretically central helping act. Generalized reciprocity, as modeled in the experimental literature, is a \emph{costly} prosocial contribution: individuals pass a benefit to an unrelated third party at real expense to themselves \citep{greiner_indirect_2005, stanca_measuring_2009, mujcic_indirect_2018}. It is the willingness to bear that cost --- not mere approval or endorsement --- that distinguishes reciprocity from low-cost signaling \citep{molm_building_2007}. Answering a stranger's question demands substantive effort and yields a public good for the community. Lower-cost gestures such as comments, upvotes, and edits show engagement, curation, or approval but do not incur the same time commitment our theoretical construct suggests. We therefore anchor the outcome on answers and report comment- and edit-inclusive alternatives in the decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}); upvotes are the one lower-cost channel no Stack Overflow data can supply, because the public dump withholds voter identity. As the decomposition shows, this choice appears reasonable: comments alone carry only a slight elevation, well below the answers increment, so reciprocity manifests the strongest in answers and broadening the outcome decreases the estimate (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Treatment.} The treatment is the receipt of an answer. We measure it with \textit{treatment}, a binary variable set to one when the user's question received, within seven days of posting, at least one answer from \emph{another} user carrying a non-negative score; the seven-day cap keeps the fixed two-day post-answer window inside the observable record. Control questions are the complement: those that received no such qualifying answer. This definition pools three cases that share the absence of timely, positively scored help from another user --- questions never answered, questions answered within seven days only by downvoted posts, and questions whose first qualifying answer arrived after seven days. We choose receiving an answer as the treatment rather than on Stack Overflow's ``accepted answer'' mechanism because accepting an answer requires the user to return to the site, and users who return are plausibly also the users who go on to help.

\paragraph{Response Time.} For each treated observation, the response-time variable used in the interaction terms is $\log(1 + \text{RT})$, where $\text{RT} = t_{\text{answer}} - t_{\text{question}}$ is the actual response time in hours: the elapsed time from the posting of the question to the arrival of the first answer with a non-negative vote score. The origin is the question itself, so RT is the true question-to-answer latency and is unaffected by the length of the pre-question window. The discrete response-time bins reported below (Table~\ref{tab:response_time_bins}) are likewise cut on this same actual response time, so a bin labelled ``1--3 days'' contains questions answered 24--72 hours after posting. For numerical stability, both response-time interaction terms ($\text{isTreatedActive} \times \log(1+\text{RT})$ and $\text{treatedPostQuestion} \times \log(1+\text{RT})$) are standardized (z-scored) and then clipped to their 5th--95th percentile range before model fitting. Consequently, the reported interaction coefficients $\gamma$ and $\delta$ are expressed per one standard deviation of the respective interaction term rather than per unit of $\log(1+\text{RT})$.

\paragraph{User Experience.} To test Hypothesis 2, we stratify our analysis by user tenure, defined as the number of days between a user's first activity on the platform and the start of the observation window. We define seven tenure buckets using right-closed intervals: $[0, 7]$, $(7, 30]$, $(30, 180]$, $(180, 365]$, $(365, 1095]$, $(1095, 2190]$, and $(2190, \infty)$ days, corresponding to the labels less than one week, one week to one month, one to six months, six to twelve months, one to three years, three to six years, and more than six years, respectively. Running separate models by bucket lets us examine how the reciprocity effect varies across the user trajectory without imposing a parametric functional form.

\subsection{Propensity Score Matching}
\label{sec:matching}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Receiving an answer (the treatment) is correlated with user characteristics: more experienced users, for instance, might ask higher-quality questions that are more likely to be answered. We therefore employ propensity score matching \citep{rosenbaum_rubin_1983} to improve the comparability of the treatment and control groups. Importantly, we match \textit{across different users}: we pair each treated question with a control question from a \emph{different} user, chosen for its similar propensity to receive an answer based on observable pre-treatment characteristics. This cross-user matching ensures that treated and control observations are independent conditional on the matched covariates, while the DiD structure within each pair absorbs residual differences.

We estimate the propensity score using pre-treatment covariates that jointly capture user experience, recent activity, and question characteristics, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:covariates}. The covariate \textit{tagAcceptShareAvg} addresses the concern that question complexity may confound the treatment: questions in tags with low accepted-answer rates are inherently more difficult, and matching on this rate ensures that treated and control questions come from similarly tractable topic areas.
Matching on the tag-level accepted-answer rate also matters for the response-time analysis, because it mitigates the concern that slower response times simply proxy for differently sized sub-communities with lower throughput. We use nearest-neighbor matching \emph{with replacement} and a caliper of 0.05 on the propensity score, requiring in addition an exact match on the calendar year, the question's primary tag, and the number of tags. Because the pool of unanswered questions is smaller than the pool of answered questions, some control questions are matched to more than one treated question; the matched file therefore contains more rows than unique question pairs (12.2 million pairs, 24.3 million rows). Each row enters the Cox analysis as an independent observation window; standard errors are discussed in the robustness appendix.

\subsection{Analytical Strategy: Cox Proportional Hazards with Time-Varying Covariates}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We model the hazard of helping using a Cox proportional hazards model with time-varying covariates. The counting-process formulation of \citet{andersen_gill_1982} extends the original Cox model \citep{cox_regression_1972} to settings with time-varying covariates and recurrent events.

For each matched pair of observations (one treated, one control), we partition the observation window into intervals at the key event times: the start of the window, the posting of the question, and the arrival of the answer (actual for treated users, synthetic for controls).

We specify the hazard function as:

\begin{multline}
\label{eq:cox}
h(t \mid \mathbf{X}(t)) = h_0(t) \exp\big(\beta_0 \cdot \text{treatment} + \beta_1 \cdot \text{phasePostQuestion}(t) + \beta_2 \cdot \text{treatedPostQuestion}(t) \\
+ \beta_3 \cdot \text{phasePostAnswer}(t) + \beta_4 \cdot \text{isTreatedActive}(t) \big)
\end{multline}

\noindent where $h_0(t)$ is the baseline hazard. \textit{treatment} is a time-constant indicator for treatment group membership (1 if the user's question received an answer, 0 otherwise); it absorbs stable between-group differences in baseline helping rates. The remaining covariates are time-varying indicators that switch on at the appropriate event times. Because \textit{treatedPostQuestion} switches on when the question is posted and remains on through the post-answer phase, the treated--control gap in the helping hazard equals $\beta_2$ during the waiting period and $\beta_2 + \beta_4$ after the answer arrives. The coefficient $\beta_4$ is therefore the difference-in-differences estimate of the change at answer arrival: the shift in the treated--control gap at the time of the answer, net of whatever divergence preceded it. We report $\beta_4$ as the treatment effect. We report the waiting-period coefficient $\beta_2$ separately, as a parallel-trends diagnostic: because no answer has yet arrived during the waiting period, it cannot reflect a response to answer receipt. It measures anticipatory or selection-driven divergence.

To test the moderation by response time (Hypothesis 3), we extend the model with two additional terms:

\begin{multline}
\label{eq:cox_speed}
h(t \mid \mathbf{X}(t)) = h_0(t) \exp\Big(\boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}(t) \\
+ \gamma \cdot \text{isTreatedActive}(t) \times \widetilde{\log}(\text{RT}) + \delta \cdot \text{treatedPostQuestion}(t) \times \widetilde{\log}(\text{RT}) \Big)
\end{multline}

\noindent where $\widetilde{\log}(\text{RT})$ denotes the standardized log response-time variable (see below), $\gamma$ captures the moderation of the answer-arrival increment by answer speed, and $\delta$ the analogous moderation during the waiting period. The logic mirrors the base model exactly. Just as $\beta_4$ alone is the treatment effect, $\gamma$ alone is the response-time moderation of that effect: it scales the arrival increment $\beta_4$, the difference-in-differences contrast on which the reciprocity effect rests. We therefore test H3 on $\gamma$. A negative $\gamma$ indicates that faster answers strengthen the arrival increment, the direction H3 predicts. The waiting-period moderation $\delta$ is the counterpart of $\beta_2$: it scales a pre-answer gap that cannot reflect a response to an answer not yet received. We report it separately as a diagnostic.

\paragraph{Stratification by Tenure.} To test Hypothesis 2, we fit separate models for each of the seven tenure buckets. This allows the baseline hazard and all coefficients to vary freely across levels of user experience, providing a nonparametric view of how reciprocity changes over the user trajectory.

\paragraph{Identification.} Our identification strategy rests on the matched DiD logic embedded in the model. Propensity score matching \citep{rosenbaum_rubin_1983} ensures that treated and control observations come from users with comparable baseline characteristics, including tenure, activity levels, and question topic difficulty. This matching absorbs between-user differences that might otherwise confound the treatment effect.

We do not include user fixed effects in the survival models. Because we match across different users (not within a user), and because the Cox model already leverages within-observation temporal variation through its time-varying covariates \citep{andersen_gill_1982}, user fixed effects are not required for identification. The DiD structure compares within-observation changes before and after the answer across treatment groups, isolating the treatment effect from stable individual differences.

The waiting-period covariate (\textit{treatedPostQuestion}) captures any lift in helping that treated users show after asking but before receiving an answer. When this term is near zero, treated and control users move in parallel up to the answer, and the arrival increment $\beta_4$ captures the response to receiving an answer rather than to asking a question. When it is not, the two groups diverge before treatment arrives; $\beta_4$ still nets out the average waiting-period gap, but we flag such strata and interpret their estimates with caution. Even a near-zero waiting-period term, however, rules out only motivational differences that materialize \textit{before} the answer. We therefore report the waiting-period term explicitly, so that we measure departures from parallel trends, and we return to its magnitude in the Results.

We analyze 24,349,760 question-level observations from 3,732,706 users on Stack Overflow, half of whose questions received at least one answer (a 50\% rate fixed by construction of the matched sample, not a platform base rate). \subsection{Generalized Reciprocity Effect (H1)}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------


To test Hypothesis~1, we estimate the pooled Cox model across all experience levels (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_experienced}). The treatment effect is the difference-in-differences increment at answer arrival ($\beta_4$); we report the waiting-period term ($\beta_2$) alongside it as a parallel-trends check. Users who received an answer went on to help others slightly more often than their matched counterparts did, and the gap is small: the arrival increment is HR\,$=$\,1.049 (95\% CI [1.044, 1.054], $p<.001$), roughly a 5\% higher rate of helping once the answer lands. The waiting-period term is small and negative ($\beta_2 =$\,$-0.007$, $p=.01$): distinguishable from zero at this sample size, but an order of magnitude smaller than the arrival increment and opposite in sign. Treated users did not diverge upward from controls before the answer arrived; if anything, they helped slightly less. The arrival increment is therefore not inflated by a pre-answer trend. A waiting-period term this close to zero indicates that asking a question did not, by itself, change how much treated users helped while they waited. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pair_bootstrap} reports matched-pair bootstrap uncertainty for the pooled model, which preserves the matched structure and confirms the model-based interval. Hypothesis~1 thus is supported. The association, however, is smaller than prior observational reports.

In response to the concern that an answers-only outcome may not reflect the full breadth of reciprocity by excluding lower-cost helping acts, Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition} re-estimates the pooled model under broader outcome definitions. The decomposition confirms that the reciprocity signal is concentrated in \emph{answers}. Comments alone show only a slight elevation (arrival HR\,$=$\,1.01, 95\% CI [1.01, 1.02]), well below the answers-only increment, and adding comments to answers dilutes rather than amplifies the estimate (answers-plus-comments arrival HR\,$=$\,1.02, [1.02, 1.03]). \subsection{Experience Moderation of Reciprocity (H2)}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

To test Hypothesis~2, we estimate the model separately within each of the seven tenure buckets (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}; Figure~\ref{fig:strength_rec}). The response is strongest at entry and fades to nothing among the most experienced, though the decline across the middle of the tenure range is not strictly monotone. The newest users respond most: in their first week, users who received an answer went on to help roughly 7.1\% more often than their matched counterparts (arrival HR\,$=$\,1.071, 95\% CI [1.056, 1.086], $p < .001$). The response then dips in the one-week-to-one-month bucket (HR\,$=$\,1.03\,[1.01, 1.05]), recovers to a plateau of about 1.05 that holds from one month through three years, and weakens again at three to six years (HR\,$=$\,1.02\,[1.01, 1.04], $p<.001$). Only users with more than six years on the platform show no response at all (HR\,$=$\,1.00\,[0.98, 1.02], n.s.). Read at this level, the pattern supports Hypothesis~2: reciprocity is a newcomer phenomenon that fades with experience. We do not read the one-week-to-one-month dip as substantively meaningful, and we stop short of describing the decline as strictly graded. Because the newcomer stratum is one of our central findings, we subject its arrival increment to a matched-pair bootstrap that resamples whole pairs and preserves the matched structure. At 500 replicates, the bootstrap 95\% CI is [1.06, 1.08], confirming the model-based interval (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pair_bootstrap}).

For newcomers, both the arrival increment ($\beta_4$) and the waiting-period term ($\beta_2$) are positive and statistically significant ($\beta_4=0.068$, $\beta_2=0.050$, both $p<.001$), whereas among veterans both are near zero. The positive waiting-period term means that newcomers begin helping more even before their answer arrives. The arrival increment nets out that divergence, but a nonzero pre-trend in this stratum is itself a caution: the design cannot fully separate that pre-answer divergence from reciprocity (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Stack Overflow was substantially disrupted by the arrival of generative-AI tools in late 2022, which sharply reduced human question-and-answer activity \citep{delriochanona_large_2024, burtch_consequences_2024}. That disruption is not the primary vantage point of this paper, but it invites a natural check: do our findings hold before and after it? Because calendar year enters the design only as an exact-matching stratum, we can re-estimate our two central results, the pooled effect (H1) and the tenure gradient (H2), separately by era (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:cohort_robustness}). The pooled effect is stable across the divide: the arrival increment is 1.04 on the pre-2020 subsample and 1.04 on the disjoint post-2020 data (95\% CI [1.03, 1.06]), against 1.05 in the full sample. The newcomer effect is not. It is present before the generative-AI era (pre-2020 arrival HR\,$=$\,1.07, matching the full sample) but not distinguishable from one afterward (post-2020 HR\,$=$\,1.00, [0.97, 1.03]), on far fewer events (31,814 versus 336,966). We read this as a genuine difference between periods: the pooled association even replicates on data that postdate the disruption, while the newcomer response is well identified in the pre- and early-generative-AI data but appears to weaken after 2020. We develop candidate explanations for why it weakens in the discussion (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Response Time Moderation (H3)}
\label{sec:rt_moderation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

To test Hypothesis~3, which predicted that faster responses strengthen the reciprocity effect, we estimate the arrival increment and its response-time moderation separately by tenure bucket (Table~\ref{tab:speed_results}); Table~\ref{tab:response_time_bins} and Figure~\ref{fig:interaction_effect} give a more flexible discrete-bin view. The evidence offers qualified support for the immediacy prediction. The continuous log-response-time interaction with the arrival increment ($\gamma$) is negative in every tenure bucket and statistically significant in all but the most senior: users who waited less for their answer responded more strongly, the direction H3 predicts. The moderation is strongest early in the trajectory (about $-0.07$ to $-0.09$ from the newcomer through one-year buckets), weakens with tenure ($-0.05$ at one to three years, $-0.02$ at three to six), and is indistinguishable from zero among users with more than six years. The discrete bins tell a consistent, if noisier, story. Users who receive help within 30 minutes barely respond at all, while those who receive help between 30 and 60 minutes respond the strongest (1.19\,[1.17, 1.21]). It declines toward one over the following day (1.06 at one to two hours, 1.02--1.05 through one day), with a smaller secondary elevation at one to three days (1.09\,[1.07, 1.11]). The pronounced early peak shows a sweet-spot pattern rather than a monotone speed gradient; we return to explanations for the sweet-spot pattern below.

The waiting-period gap carries a response-time moderation of its own ($\delta$), positive in every bucket --- the counterpart of the $\beta_2$ diagnostic, capturing how the \emph{pre-answer} treated--control gap varies with answer speed. Because no answer has arrived yet, we read it as anticipation or selection, not as a response to answer speed. It is separate from $\gamma$, the moderation of the arrival increment: the difference-in-differences nets the pre-answer gap out of the increment, so the moderation we report ($\gamma$) reflects the change at answer arrival, not treated users' pre-answer trajectories.

A natural concern is that response time reflects unobserved answer quality. People who answer within minutes tend to post something terse or link-only, whereas those who take longer tend to write fuller, more detailed answers. So, what prompts users to reciprocate may be the quality of the answer they received, not its timing. If quality and response time are correlated in this way, the sweet-spot pattern could reflect quality differences rather than immediacy. We probe the threat with two checks.

The first is the direct test for the response-time pattern: we re-estimate each response-time bin with the three answer-quality controls (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:response_time_bins_quality}). Vote score and acceptance are post-treatment, and acceptance in particular requires the asker to return to the platform, so conditioning on them absorbs some of the effect itself, not only the quality confound. The clearest sign of this over-adjustment is that under the controls the fastest bins fall significantly \emph{below} one (0.95), which has no plausible causal reading. Even so, the 30--60-minute peak falls only from 1.19 to 1.03 (95\% CI [1.02, 1.04], $p<.001$) and remains the significant early-window maximum, and the one-to-three-day elevation persists (1.09). The sweet-spot pattern remains even after an adjustment likely biased against it.

The second check concerns the pooled \emph{level} of the arrival increment. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:answer_quality_robustness} reports how it shifts as the same controls enter one at a time. Answer length, which is the least endogenous signal and plausibly correlated with answer quality, barely moves it (from 1.04 to 1.03). The more endogenous vote-score and acceptance signals pull it further, to 1.01 and then below one (0.98); another sensitivity check, where we account for the heavy right tail of vote scores, accompanies these fits (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:score_coding_sensitivity}). As above, these level estimates are bounds, not estimates of the effect net of quality: conditioning on post-treatment signals likely takes away from the effect itself. The genuine residual caveat is that our quality measures are coarse, so we cannot cleanly separate the effects of timing and quality, and cannot rule out that answer quality, imperfectly measured, remains a rival to a timing-based reading of the response-time pattern.

One interpretation of the sweet-spot pattern is \emph{re-engagement}: users who post a question may remain actively engaged while waiting during the ongoing session on the platform, elevating helping during the waiting period regardless of whether an answer arrives. On this account, a fast answer might lead users to end their session and, with it, their engagement without returning to the platform. If an answer arrives after they have ended their session, they might return to look at the answer and reciprocate in the newly started session triggered by the answer. Later answers are more likely to re-engage the user in another session, which answers in the same initial session do not. 

Figure~\ref{fig:help_rate_adoption_pooled} provides descriptive context, comparing users who have received an answer (red) with users who will receive one but are still waiting (blue), alongside matched controls. In the first half-hour after the question --- when only the fastest answers have arrived --- users still waiting help at the highest rate, consistent with the idea that users help while waiting on the platform. From roughly an hour onward, the ordering flips: users who have received an answer help at a modestly higher rate than those still waiting and than controls, consistent with a small post-answer elevation. We read this as descriptive evidence for the re-engagement mechanism. Figure~\ref{fig:help_rate_adoption_by_tenure} disaggregates this comparison by tenure bucket and shows the same qualitative pattern in every stratum. These raw-rate orderings are descriptive: the differences are small, and answer-arrival timing is not random within the treated group, so we do not read them as decisive evidence for any one mechanism. Still, they are consistent with the modest reciprocity effect and the re-engagement mechanism the models estimate.

Overall, however, re-engagement is speculative since we do not observe session boundaries (logins, logouts, inactivity gaps). We present it as one candidate among several, not as an established mechanism. We return to this question in Section~\ref{sec:rt_discussion}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Methodological Contribution}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Researchers who study generalized reciprocity from platform data face a persistent difficulty: generally active users are more likely to ask questions and post answers. A correlation between help received and help given can therefore reflect nothing more than how active a user is. Prior observational studies that report positive associations without accounting for this selection \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_engaging_2018, joyce_predicting_2006} may have overstated the role of reciprocity.

Our matched difference-in-differences survival design addresses that confound. It compares changes in helping, before and after a question is posted, between users whose questions received answers and propensity-score-matched users whose questions did not. Entering the waiting period as its own term makes any pre-answer divergence visible, which turns parallel trends into something a reader can inspect rather than something we assert. The estimate this yields is small and concentrated among newcomers, and it is substantially smaller than many earlier reports --- which is what activity confounding in those designs would produce.

The design travels past this particular question. Any platform event that arrives at a time the researcher does not control --- a first review, a moderator intervention, a badge, a reply --- raises the same confound, and the same comparison of matched users before and after the event addresses it. We therefore offer the design, and not only the estimate, as a contribution.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reciprocity as a Contributor-Recruitment Mechanism}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Users respond to being helped less the longer they have been on the platform. The newest respond most (HR\,$=$\,1.07) and the most experienced not at all (arrival HR\,$=$\,1.00; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). Read against the theory, this gradient does more than qualify the reciprocity account. It relocates it. Generalized reciprocity on this platform is not the general engine of cooperation among established members that the literature often takes it to be. It is a channel that works at the point of entry and closes as users settle in.

Why it closes is what the theory has to explain. The norm-displacement account developed in Section~\ref{sec:trajectory} offers one answer: as users gain experience, the community's own incentives crowd out a general reciprocal impulse. Experienced users on Stack Overflow have reasons to help that newcomers do not yet have --- reputation, visible status, a stake in the community. That the most experienced users do not respond at all fits this account. Our design tracks what users did rather than what moved them, so we cannot confirm it.

The reframing also makes sense of an inconsistent literature. Researchers who sample mostly experienced users, or who pool across experience levels dominated by experienced contributors, should find weaker reciprocity effects. Those who catch users early should find stronger ones. Prior findings may diverge, then, not because anyone erred, but because reciprocity is not a fixed property of a population.

What the gradient does not tell us is what takes reciprocity's place. Our analysis establishes that the short-run response declines with experience; it does not characterize the motives that replace it. The theoretical argument implicates community-specific norms and status concerns, but testing that account needs direct measures of them --- surveys of experienced contributors, or experiments that manipulate reputation visibility and observe whether helping follows.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Response-Time Pattern and Its Interpretation}
\label{sec:rt_discussion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The response-time analysis tested a prediction drawn from laboratory studies of gratitude: that faster help produces stronger reciprocity. The evidence gives it qualified support. The continuous interaction runs in the predicted direction in every tenure bucket, and the discrete bins locate the response in an early window, peaking for answers that arrive between 30 and 60 minutes. That much fits an emotional-immediacy account, in which help arriving while the need is still felt draws a stronger response. The fastest answers do not fit it. A pure immediacy account gives no reason why answers within 30 minutes should do nothing at all.

Three readings of that anomaly compete, and our data separate none of them cleanly. The first is answer quality: the quickest replies may also be the thinnest, which the Results adjudicate as far as the dump allows (Section~\ref{sec:rt_moderation}). The second is re-engagement: an answer that lands while a user is still on the site may end their visit, while one that lands later brings them back. The third is question characteristics: response time stands in part for properties of the question that the tag-level accepted-answer rate does not capture, since questions that draw fast answers may differ from those that wait. We advance all three as conjectures. We observe neither session boundaries nor question difficulty, and our quality signals are coarse.

The pattern bears only weakly on the norm-displacement account. Among the most experienced users, the arrival increment and its response-time moderation are both near zero, which is consistent with the mechanism having faded rather than merely weakened. We do not lean on response time as evidence for displacement.

Settling the interpretation needs data the archive does not hold. Session-level records would test re-engagement directly. Text-based measures of question complexity or specificity would separate timing from difficulty. Randomly assigned answer timing would separate both from immediacy. Each of these is available to a platform or an experimenter, and none of them to us.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implications for Platform Design}
\label{sec:design_implications}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Because the response we observe is modest and concentrated among newcomers, designers can use it mainly to recruit new contributors rather than to keep established members contributing.

Designers who want to grow their contributor base should therefore make sure that new users get answers to their questions. Newcomers who received an answer went on to help about 7\% more often than their matched counterparts (HR\,$=$\,1.07; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}), or roughly 8 additional helping events per 1{,}000 answered newcomer questions (Table~\ref{tab:absolute_effects}). The absolute figure is small because helping is rare in this population to begin with: matched newcomer controls average about 0.11 helping events per observation window, and most control observations record none at all. Put differently, about 125 newcomer questions must be answered to produce one additional act of helping. This is still the highest yield of any tenure group, next to the statistically indistinguishable one-to-twelve-month strata (roughly 126--128 questions) but clearly more favorable than the pooled figure of about 158. The case for concentrating on newcomers therefore rests both on their being the group that responds most and on a per-answer yield at least as favorable as any other group's. The most experienced users do not respond (arrival HR\,$=$\,1.00), so answering their questions buys a designer nothing. Designers thus have most leverage with reciprocity at the point of entry.

\input{analysis/output_tables/absolute_effects.tex}

Response speed is a weaker basis for design than it first appears. Users who received an answer more quickly generally responded more strongly, and those answered between 30 and 60 minutes responded the most (Tables~\ref{tab:speed_results} and~\ref{tab:response_time_bins}). But the effect is small, and the pattern admits two readings that point designers in different directions. We therefore hand designers two questions to run on their own platforms rather than a lever to pull.

The first question is how answers land in a user's session. Platforms record what our data do not: when a user arrives, how long they stay, and whether an answer reaches them inside that visit or after it has ended. If the sweet spot reflects re-engagement, an answer that arrives mid-visit competes with the activity a user is already engaged in, while one that arrives after the visit gives them a reason to come back. Designers would then be working on the return trip rather than the clock. What matters is when the notification lands, whether the answer is still visible when the user next opens the platform, and how prominently it is surfaced. Time to first answer would be the wrong thing to optimize.

The second question is whether fast answers are \emph{low-cost contributions}: replies written in minutes that are terse or link-only, and that clear the queue without doing much for the asker. The two fastest bins are indistinguishable from no effect (0.998 and 1.004; Table~\ref{tab:response_time_bins}), which is what we would expect if the quickest replies are also the thinnest. A newcomer whose first exchange with the community is a curt reply may simply not return. If low-cost contributions drive the fastest bin, the design problem sits in quality at the fast end rather than in delay, and platforms would do better to discourage quick throwaway replies to newcomers than to shorten the queue further. Our own checks speak to this only partly (Section~\ref{sec:rt_moderation}); the quality signals in the public dump are too coarse to settle it. Platforms that observe their own answer ratings and newcomer retention can settle it directly.

Both readings lead to the same caution. Designers stand on firmer ground making sure that newcomers' questions get answered at all, and that users notice when they do, than tuning for a particular response window.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}
\label{sec:limitations}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Four questions bound what this study can claim: what we measure, what we can identify, what we cannot see, and where the estimates apply. We take them in turn, and each ends where the next study would begin.

Start with what we measure. Our helping outcome is the posting of an answer to another user's question, which we treat as costly public contribution. Reciprocity can also run through channels we do not observe. On the platform, upvotes are the clearest case: the public dump withholds the voter's identity, so votes given cannot be attributed to a user at all. Off the platform, a helped user might answer a colleague directly, write the solution up elsewhere, or pass it on somewhere Stack Overflow never records. Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition} sets out which channels the outcome does and does not include. If newcomers reciprocate through these unobserved channels more than veterans do, our estimates understate reciprocity, and understate it most in the stratum where we find it strongest, which makes the tenure gradient a conservative one.

Turn next to what we can identify. Our estimate of reciprocity is the arrival increment, the change in helping at the moment an answer arrives. It is identified under several assumptions, but one does the work: that nothing else which raises helping changes at that same moment.

Three kinds of selection bear on that assumption. The matching handles selection on \emph{observables}, the characteristics that predict getting answered, and leaves every covariate below conventional balance thresholds (Figure~\ref{fig:love_plot}). The difference-in-differences removes selection on \emph{stable unobservables}, a lasting disposition to both attract answers and give help, since each user is measured against their own baseline. The third kind remains: something that changes about the user inside the window and moves both outcomes at once. Someone who becomes more active may draw an answer sooner and, for the same reason, help more. The waiting-period term catches only what surfaces before the answer lands; whatever emerges alongside it sits inside the arrival increment, where we cannot separate it from reciprocity.

This bears hardest on the tenure gradient. To produce it rather than simply raise the pooled effect, the shift would have to be stronger among newcomers than among veterans. We cannot rule that out, and newcomers are the one stratum where the pre-answer term is itself positive and significant. Settling it needs a design that makes answer arrival exogenous: a field experiment that seeds answers to a random subset of questions, or a platform change that shifts answering capacity for reasons unrelated to who is asking.

The clearest version of that third problem has a name: \emph{social expectation}. Getting answered makes a user visible. The answerer is watching, others read the thread, and the asker's profile draws traffic. Helping afterwards could then be a response to being watched rather than to being helped.

Two things would have to hold for social expectation to explain our results away. Visibility must rise when the answer arrives, over and above the rise at question posting, since the waiting-period term already nets the latter out. And the rise must bind newcomers more tightly than veterans, or it cannot produce the tenure gradient.

The first condition is plausible. An answered question moves up in feeds and draws more traffic than one sitting unanswered. The second is where the account strains. Veterans have more reputation at stake and more visible profiles, so an audience should move them at least as much, yet they do not respond at all. Getting the gradient out of social expectation needs the further assumption that newcomers are more sensitive to being watched, which is plausible but a separate claim our design does not test.

Social expectation may therefore explain part of the arrival increment, and we can say neither how much nor in which direction. Being answered cuts both ways: it lifts a question in activity-sorted feeds for a while, but it also takes the question out of the unanswered queue that potential answerers browse. What social expectation threatens is why users help, not whether their behavior changes when an answer arrives. Testing it needs a measure of how visible a question was at the time, which the dump does not provide: view counts arrive as one cumulative total at the collection date, mixing a question's age, its topic's popularity, and years of later search traffic. Time-stamped view data would settle it.

That brings us to what we cannot see. The design captures a change in behavior but not the psychology behind it. Gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement, and re-engagement are all \textit{proposed} interpretations, each consistent with part of the pattern and none identified by our data. We observe no session boundaries, no reputation salience, and no emotional states. Separating these pathways calls for behavioral records paired with measures taken while people are on the platform: experience sampling of emotional state, or direct measures of how users reason about obligation when they return to a thread.

Finally, where the estimates apply. Stack Overflow is a large, relatively anonymous, gamified platform built around technical question-answering. The patterns we estimate may not transfer to smaller or relation-based communities (enterprise Slack channels, academic peer review, intimate hobby forums) where social ties and non-status motives differ. We therefore refrain from claiming that generalized reciprocity ``likely operates'' elsewhere; at most, our estimates describe one platform's pre-/early-generative-AI era. Matching narrows the scope once more, to answered questions that have a comparable unanswered counterpart; Appendix~\ref{sec:estimand_scope} reports which questions this leaves out and why the restriction runs in a conservative direction.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generalized Reciprocity in the Generative-AI Era}
\label{sec:genai}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Our analysis window runs from Stack Overflow's inception through 1 April 2025, and it should be read as a \textit{historical snapshot} rather than a guide to contemporary platform design. Since generative-AI tools arrived in late 2022, the ecosystem has been disrupted: question volume and human-to-human answering have both declined sharply \citep{delriochanona_large_2024, burtch_consequences_2024}. Our own data carry a trace of it. The per-window helping rate is markedly lower after 2020 than before, about 0.057 against 0.164 helping events per matched window.

This bears on what our estimates describe. Because calendar year is an exact-matching stratum, the pre-2020 period supplies most of the analysis, which dates our results to a platform where community answering was still the norm. The two central findings then behave differently across the divide, and the difference is informative in itself. The pooled effect survives it and replicates on the post-2020 data. The newcomer response does not: it is present before 2020 and not distinguishable from zero afterward (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:cohort_robustness}). The recruitment channel we identify, in other words, weakened at about the point the platform began to change.

Why it weakened is a question our data cannot answer, and we offer two conjectures rather than a conclusion. One is composition: the newcomers who still join after ChatGPT's release are a selected group, and the post-2022 exit fell hardest on the junior users who carried the earlier response \citep{burtch_consequences_2024}. The other is opportunity: activity declined most in the topics where generative-AI tools perform best \citep{delriochanona_large_2024}, so the questions that remain may skew harder, leaving a newly helped newcomer fewer questions they are able to answer. Both would weaken the recruitment channel for reasons outside the reciprocity mechanism itself. Neither is testable here, since we observe neither question difficulty over time nor who chose not to join.

The larger question is what becomes of reciprocal recruitment when a machine supplies the answer. If newcomers increasingly get help from an assistant rather than a person, the human exchange that gave them their modest push into contributing thins out, and it thins out exactly where we find the mechanism working. The demand for human contribution does not disappear with routine question-answering, however. Systems still depend on people for correction, feedback, and the ground-truth detail that no model generates on its own. Whether generalized reciprocity can recruit those contributors when the help that triggers it comes from a machine is a question this design can be re-run to answer as the data accumulate.

%=============================================================
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion} %=============================================================
Generalized reciprocity is widely credited with sustaining cooperation on knowledge-sharing platforms, yet observational tests run into a stubborn confound: generally active users ask and answer more questions at the same time. Our matched difference-in-differences survival design confronts that confound directly and delivers a cleaner short-run test of whether people help more once their own questions are answered. The response to being helped is strongest among newcomers and fades to nothing among the longest-tenured users. That gradient points to a reframing: on this platform, generalized reciprocity works less as a general engine of cooperation among established members than as a channel for recruiting contributors at the point of entry. This continues to matter as generative-AI systems absorb routine question-answering. People still supply what machines cannot generate on their own: feedback, input, correction, and the ground-truth knowledge that populates shared resources, such as local detail added to mapping platforms. For much of this, these systems in fact depend on the people who provide it. Understanding how such contributors are recruited therefore grows more important, not less. Our study contributes both a finding and a framework: the recruitment leverage of generalized reciprocity concentrates at the point of entry, and our design offers a re-runnable way to measure and strengthen it wherever human contribution still matters.
