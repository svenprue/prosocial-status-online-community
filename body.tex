\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Whether receiving help on an online platform motivates subsequent helping behavior has been a persistent question in the study of online communities. Generalized reciprocity, the tendency to help others after being helped oneself \citep{nowak_upstream_2007, baker_paying_2014}, is among the mechanisms proposed to sustain cooperation on platforms like Stack Overflow and Wikipedia, where direct exchange between the same two individuals is rare. If receiving help reliably increases the probability of giving it, cooperation may sustain itself through cascading helping acts, even among strangers \citep{fowler_cooperative_2010}. The behavioral evidence, however, is inconsistent: some observational studies find substantial reciprocity effects \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_why_2019}, while others find weak or null relationships \citep{wasko_why_2005, joyce_predicting_2006}. We argue that much of this inconsistency is methodological. Our contribution is a better-identified short-run behavioral test on one platform: whether helping rises after answer receipt. The data do not directly measure the psychological mechanisms that theory proposes (gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement); we test only the behavioral consequence those mechanisms predict.

Observational studies of reciprocity on platforms face a persistent confound: more engaged users are more likely both to seek help and to provide it, regardless of whether their own questions are resolved. Studies that correlate help received with help given, without accounting for this baseline activity, conflate reciprocity with general user engagement \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_engaging_2018, joyce_predicting_2006}. A related limitation is that prior designs have largely treated receiving help as a one-time event, even though help-seeking and helping occur continuously.

We address both problems by developing a matched difference-in-differences (DiD) survival analysis, applied to over 21 million questions from Stack Overflow. For each question a user posts, we define observation windows before and after the question and compare changes in helping behavior between users whose question received an answer (treated) and propensity-score-matched users whose question did not (control). We use Cox proportional hazards models to account for the continuous-time dynamics of helping behavior and the staggered, time-varying structure of treatment.

We develop two theoretical predictions alongside the basic reciprocity hypothesis. Drawing on theories of socialization and norm internalization, we predict that the reciprocity effect is stronger among newcomers than among experienced users (H2). Newcomers have not yet internalized community-specific norms or learned to value platform incentives such as reputation points; general moral obligations to reciprocate are therefore more likely to drive their behavior. As users accumulate experience, these general-purpose triggers may be displaced by community-specific motivations. We also predict that faster responses amplify the reciprocity effect (H3), because gratitude is more intense when help arrives while the need is still salient \citep{mccullough_is_2001, tsang_gratitude_2006}.

The results support the baseline reciprocity hypothesis (H1) in a modest, newcomer-concentrated form, and offer qualified support for the tenure gradient (H2) and the immediacy pattern (H3). Relative to matched controls and to their own pre-question baseline, users who receive an answer show a small elevation in their subsequent answering of other users' questions: the pooled difference-in-differences increment at answer arrival is HR\,$=$\,1.03 (95\% CI [1.02, 1.05], $p<.001$). The association is steeply graded by tenure: the arrival increment is largest among newcomers (HR\,$=$\,[[AO:nc-arrival-hr]] in the first week, $p < .001$) and fades to zero among the longest-tenured users. Our estimand throughout is the increment at answer arrival ($\beta_4$), the difference-in-differences contrast that nets out any treated--control divergence arising before the answer; we report the waiting-period term ($\beta_2$) alongside it as an explicit parallel-trends diagnostic rather than folding it into the effect, and we re-estimate the model under broader outcome definitions that add lower-cost helping acts (comments, edits) in a decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}) to show where the signal resides.

Our study makes three contributions. First, using a design built to remove the activity confound that inflates observational estimates, we provide a better-identified short-run behavioral test: receiving an answer is associated with a small increase in subsequent helping on Stack Overflow (arrival-increment HR\,$=$\,1.03), substantially smaller than prior observational reports and [[AO:pooled-quality-direction --- attenuated/reversed]] once answer-quality controls are added. Second, we show that this association is steeply concentrated among newcomers and absent among veterans --- the tenure gradient is the most robust and informative pattern in the data, though we cannot cleanly separate reciprocity from selection into being answered. Third, methodologically, we decompose the outcome by help type and enter the waiting period as an explicit term, making pre-answer divergence visible rather than silently absorbed into the treatment effect.

\section{Theory}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generalized Reciprocity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A central mechanism proposed to support cooperation in online communities is \textit{generalized reciprocity}, the tendency for individuals who have received help to help others in turn, even when the recipient is not the original benefactor \citep{nowak_upstream_2007, fowler_cooperative_2010}. Unlike direct reciprocity, it requires no repeated exchange with the same person: it operates as a pay-it-forward principle, sustaining collective contributions in groups --- such as online knowledge-sharing platforms --- where interactions are infrequent and non-recurring.

Socio-psychological research identifies two complementary mechanisms underlying generalized reciprocity: moral reasoning and emotional response. On the moral side, individuals who reason by universalization --- asking what would happen if all members requested more help than they gave --- conclude that widespread free-riding would undermine the community as a source of help, generating a shared obligation to reciprocate toward the group \citep{levine_logic_2020, curry_is_2019}. On the emotional side, gratitude aroused by the perceived generosity of others motivates prosocial action beyond direct reciprocation \citep{mccullough_is_2001, tsang_gratitude_2006, bartlett_gratitude_2006} and is especially relevant where free riding is easy, as on knowledge-sharing platforms \citep{molm_building_2007, elster_norms_2009}. These moral and emotional pathways are the \textit{proposed} generative mechanisms behind generalized reciprocity; our design tests only whether helping rises after answer receipt, not whether gratitude or moral obligation is experienced.

Evidence that generalized reciprocity can operate --- and can initiate cascading acts of generosity \citep{fowler_cooperative_2010, boyd_evolution_1989} --- is robust: comparative research indicates that the underlying norm is globally shared \citep{curry_is_2019}, and experimental demonstrations span laboratory \citep{greiner_indirect_2005, molm_building_2007, stanca_measuring_2009} and field settings \citep{mujcic_indirect_2018, fowler_cooperative_2010}. The open question is how strongly the mechanism operates in situ on large platforms --- a question observational data have answered inconsistently, for reasons we argue are largely methodological.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Empirical Challenges in Measuring Generalized Reciprocity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

One challenge is the reliance on self-reported motives in surveys and interviews. Contributors frequently describe reciprocity as a central reason for their engagement \citep{wasko_it_2000, lakhani_how_2003}, yet people have difficulty articulating the moral motives behind their actions \citep{vaisey_motivation_2009, mcclelland_how_1989}, and introspection struggles to separate reciprocity from altruism, conformity to perceived norms, status pursuit, or the popular rhetoric of ``giving back'' as post-hoc rationalization \citep{batson1991altruism, benabou_incentives_2006}. Self-reported data may therefore overstate the prevalence of reciprocity as a discrete motive.

The more fundamental challenge is the endogeneity of help-seeking and help-giving in observational data. Users who ask questions on platforms like Stack Overflow are also disproportionately likely to answer questions --- not because receiving help triggered a reciprocal response, but because both behaviors reflect the same underlying characteristics, such as greater platform engagement. Studies that correlate help received with help given without accounting for this baseline activity effectively confound reciprocity with user type \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_engaging_2018, joyce_predicting_2006}. This confound is the central identification challenge that motivates the matched difference-in-differences design we develop in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

Prior observational studies of online reciprocity report mixed magnitudes. \citet{baker_paying_2014} and \citet{chen_why_2019} find positive associations between help received and subsequent helping; \citet{wasko_why_2005} and \citet{joyce_predicting_2006} find weak or null relationships. Our matched DiD estimates sit below the larger positive reports (pooled arrival-increment HR\,$=$\,1.03; newcomer arrival increment $\approx$\,[[AO:nc-arrival-hr]]), consistent with the view that designs that do not net out baseline activity overstate the effect. The tenure gradient --- clear elevation among newcomers, null among veterans --- offers one reconciliation of the mixed prior findings.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reciprocity and the User Trajectory}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We propose that the mixed findings in the literature can be partly reconciled by attending to a dimension that prior research has largely overlooked: the user trajectory from newcomer to experienced contributor. We argue that generalized reciprocity is strongest for new users and attenuates as users gain experience on the platform.

The logic of this prediction draws on theories of socialization and norm internalization. When a user first encounters an online community, they are unfamiliar with its specific norms, social structures, and reward systems. At this stage, community-specific incentives, such as reputation points, badges, or leaderboard positions, carry little motivational weight because the newcomer has not yet learned to value them. Instead, initial contributions are guided by more general moral principles that operate independently of community-specific knowledge. Generalized reciprocity is precisely such a principle: it requires only that the individual has received help and feels a moral obligation or emotional impulse to help in return, without any need to understand or identify with the particular community \citep{levine_logic_2020, curry_is_2019}.

As users interact with the community, they gradually learn its norms and develop a sense of membership. On platforms like Stack Overflow, these norms are codified in explicit reputation systems that reward helpful behavior with visible status markers. Once users internalize these community-specific norms, the broad moral obligation of reciprocity may be displaced by more localized incentive structures --- a theoretical prediction our design cannot adjudicate directly, because we do not observe reputation salience or user motives. The logic of universalization (``I should help because I was helped'') gives way to the logic of community membership (``I should help because that is what valued members do''). This norm displacement has been theorized in related contexts by \citet{benabou_incentives_2006}, who show how external incentives can crowd out moral motives.

Two further mechanisms accelerate this decline. As users accumulate experiences of receiving help, each additional instance becomes less surprising, and the gratitude that fuels reciprocity habituates \citep{mccullough_adaptation_2008}. And as users learn that the platform rewards helping with reputation and status, they may increasingly attribute others' helpfulness to status pursuit rather than genuine generosity, further dampening that gratitude.

This account predicts that generalized reciprocity is strongest among newcomers and declines with experience. This also helps explain why prior studies have reached different conclusions about the importance of reciprocity: those that focus on or oversample experienced users may underestimate reciprocity, while those that capture newcomer behavior may find stronger effects.

\hypmark{hyp:1}
\begin{quote}
\textsc{Hypothesis 1 (H1).} \emph{Receiving help increases a user's propensity to help others (generalized reciprocity effect).}
\end{quote}

\hypmark{hyp:2}
\begin{quote}
\textsc{Hypothesis 2 (H2).} \emph{The generalized reciprocity effect is strongest among newcomers and decreases as users gain experience on the platform.}
\end{quote}

The stakes of this prediction extend beyond reconciling mixed findings. If the reciprocity association is concentrated among newcomers, generalized reciprocity would function less as a general engine of cooperation among established members than as a \textit{contributor-recruitment} channel operating at the point of entry, before community-specific incentives take hold --- a design implication we develop in Section~\ref{sec:design_implications}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Response Time and the Immediacy of Reciprocity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The emotional mechanisms underlying reciprocity suggest that the speed of help matters for its strength. Gratitude, the primary proposed emotional driver of generalized reciprocity \citep{mccullough_is_2001}, is triggered by the perception of a benefactor's generosity, and its intensity is likely to depend on the characteristics of the helping experience --- including its timeliness.

A swift response can amplify the experience of receiving help: a fast answer signals that the community is attentive and generous, and it arrives while the asker's need is still salient, when gratitude should be most intense. Conversely, when an answer arrives hours or days later, the original need may have faded or been met elsewhere, the associative link between asking and receiving weakens, and the delay itself may signal an unresponsive community --- attenuating the emotional and moral responses that underpin reciprocation.

These considerations lead to a straightforward prediction:

\hypmark{hyp:3}
\begin{quote}
\textsc{Hypothesis 3 (H3).} \emph{Faster responses to a user's question strengthen the generalized reciprocity effect, while slower responses weaken it.}
\end{quote}

We note that this hypothesis follows from the dominant theoretical accounts of generalized reciprocity, which emphasize emotional immediacy and the salience of the helping experience. However, as we will show, field settings introduce structural dynamics, particularly the behavioral consequences of users being online and offline, that qualify the predictions of laboratory-based theory. We return to this issue when presenting our results.

%======================================================================
\section{Empirical Setting: Stack Overflow}
\label{sec:empirical_setting}
%======================================================================

Stack Overflow is well suited to examining generalized reciprocity in online knowledge sharing. The platform provides publicly available quarterly data dumps containing longitudinal records of user behavior, including knowledge-seeking and knowledge-providing activities dating back to the platform's inception. These data allow us to reconstruct helping patterns over time and to analyze the temporal sequence of receiving and providing help on which any study of generalized reciprocity depends. Stack Overflow's active question-and-answer volume has declined sharply since the release of generative AI tools in late 2022; our sample spans platform inception through 1 April 2025 and therefore captures a period that includes but predates the steepest LLM-driven decline. We treat the findings as a historical snapshot of one large, technical, relatively anonymous, status-driven platform; external validity to other communities is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

At its core, Stack Overflow is a question-and-answer platform for software developers and technologists. As of mid-2025, the site had accumulated over 24 million questions and 36 million answers from more than 4.5 million users. Its user base spans a wide range of experience levels, from learners to professional software developers \citep{stack_overflow_stack_2024}. Questions on the platform must be tightly focused on specific programming problems; broad or opinion-based inquiries are typically closed by community moderation \citep{stack_exchange_inc_how_2024}. The most frequently discussed topics, as reflected in question tags, include JavaScript, Python, Java, C\#, PHP, Android, and HTML, spanning areas from web development and mobile applications to data science and machine learning. Stack Overflow is built around several core features: tags categorize questions by topic, facilitating expertise matching; a voting system allows users to upvote or downvote questions and answers to assess quality; and reputation points are earned for valued contributions such as providing high-quality answers \citep{stack_exchange_inc_how_2024}.

For our study, the key feature is the question-and-answer interaction between users. When a user posts a question, other users may provide answers. This creates a timestamped sequence (question posted, answer received) that we exploit to measure the temporal dynamics of generalized reciprocity. On Stack Overflow, question-askers and answerers are typically different individuals, which makes the platform well suited to studying \textit{generalized} reciprocity: users who receive help from one community member may subsequently help entirely different members.

%======================================================================
\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
%======================================================================

To test our predictions, we develop a matched staggered difference-in-differences survival analysis that leverages the temporal structure of help-seeking and help-giving on Stack Overflow. Our design compares changes in helping behavior (measured as the hazard of posting an answer) before and after a user receives an answer, between users who receive an answer and matched users who do not. This approach integrates two methodological traditions: the staggered difference-in-differences framework of \citet{callaway_santanna_2021}, which accommodates variation in treatment timing and treatment effect heterogeneity, and the counting-process formulation of the Cox proportional hazards model \citep{andersen_gill_1982}, which allows treatment status and other covariates to vary continuously over the observation window.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Design}
\label{sec:study_design}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For each question posted by a user, we construct a timeline centered on two key events: the posting of the question ($T_{\text{question}}$) and, for questions that receive an answer, the time at which the first answer with a non-negative vote score is posted ($T_{\text{answer}}$). We define an observation window spanning the two days before the question is posted ($T_{\text{question}} - 2\text{D}$) through the two days after ($T_{\text{question}} + 2\text{D}$). This yields a 4-day timeline for each question, divided into distinct phases.

We define three phases within each observation window (see Figure~\ref{fig:study_design}). The first phase, the \textit{pre-question phase}, is from the start of the observation window to the posting of the question ($T_{\text{question}} - 2\text{D}$ to $T_{\text{question}}$). This phase captures baseline helping activity before the focal question is asked.
The second phase, the \textit{waiting period}, is from the posting of the question to the receipt of the first answer ($T_{\text{question}}$ to $T_{\text{answer}}$). During this interval, the user has asked for help but has not yet received it. 
The final \textit{post-answer phase} is from the receipt of the answer to the end of the observation window ($T_{\text{answer}}$ to $T_{\text{question}} + 2\text{D}$). This is the phase in which the reciprocity effect, if it exists, should manifest.

A key feature of our design is that the post-answer (treated) phase begins at different elapsed times across observations, depending on how quickly the posted question receives a response.

This staggered timing of the post-answer phase corresponds to the setting studied by \citet{callaway_santanna_2021}, who show that standard two-way fixed-effects estimators can be biased when treatment timing varies, and treatment effects are heterogeneous. Our approach avoids this pitfall by explicitly modeling the time-varying change in treatment status within each observation window and by comparing treated observations only to not-yet-treated or never-treated controls.

For control users (those whose questions did not receive an answer), there is no natural transition between the waiting period and the post-answer phase. To maintain temporal alignment with the matched treated observation, we assign the control observation a synthetic phase transition at the same elapsed time as the treated user's response time. That is, if the treated user's answer was posted 4 hours after their question, the matched control observation's timeline is split at $T_{\text{question}} + 4\text{h}$, creating an aligned ``post'' phase of equal duration. This ensures that any differences between treated and control observations in the post-answer phase are not driven by differences in the length of the observation period.

The survival clock in our Cox models is centered on posting of the respective question: time zero corresponds to the start of the observation window ($T_{\text{question}} - 2\text{D}$), so that the question itself is posted at day~2 of the timeline and the observation window closes at day~4. All help events (answers posted by the focal user) are recorded with their exact timestamps and converted to elapsed time on this clock.

Within each timeline, we track all instances in which the user provides an answer to another user's question. These helping events constitute the outcome in our survival analysis; broader outcome definitions that also count comments and edits are examined in the decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}). Conceptually, our design asks: Does the hazard of helping increase after a user receives an answer, relative to the change in hazard for a matched user who does not receive an answer? We present descriptive evidence on the raw help rates over this timeline in Section~\ref{sec:results} (Figure~\ref{fig:help_rate_pooled}), before turning to the formal Cox model estimates.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We used the full sample of non-deleted questions from Stack Overflow's inception on 31 July 2008 through 1 April 2025. We addressed two edge cases that would render our fixed-length observation window invalid. First, for early platform questions, the pre-period could predate the platform's launch, meaning no answers were possible at that time. We removed 1,080 such cases. Second, due to the data dump's 1 April 2025 cutoff, the post-period could be prematurely truncated. We excluded 8,029 cases affected by this. We then excluded 2,671,121 questions that received an answer from the question poster. These filtering steps resulted in a pre-matching dataset of 21,033,429 questions from 4,764,048 unique users. After propensity score matching (described below), the matched file comprises 24,349,760 question-level rows (12,174,880 matched pairs; some control questions appear more than once) from 3,732,706 unique users, with a 50\% treatment rate by construction. All matched rows enter the Cox analysis; the helping outcome is the posting of an answer to another user's question (self-directed accepts excluded), yielding 3,435,973 helping events in the pooled analysis sample (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_experienced}).

\paragraph{Outcome.} The event of interest is the posting of an answer to another user's question --- the platform's central helping act. Within each window we track when (and whether) the focal user answers any other user's question, and the Cox model estimates the instantaneous hazard of this helping event as a function of time-varying covariates that capture the phase of the observation timeline and the treatment status. Self-directed acts (accepting an answer on one's own question) are excluded. To address the concern that answers alone may understate lower-cost reciprocity, we re-estimate the pooled model under broader outcome definitions --- comments only, edits only, and composites that add these channels to answers --- and report the full decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}.

\paragraph{Why answers anchor the construct.} We treat the posting of an answer as the theoretically central helping act. Generalized reciprocity, as modeled in the experimental literature, is a \emph{costly} prosocial contribution: individuals pass a benefit to an unrelated third party at real expense to themselves \citep{greiner_indirect_2005, stanca_measuring_2009, mujcic_indirect_2018}, and it is the willingness to bear that cost --- not mere approval or endorsement --- that distinguishes reciprocity from low-cost signaling \citep{molm_building_2007}. Answering a stranger's question is the platform analogue: it demands substantive effort and yields a public good for the community. Lower-cost gestures such as comments, upvotes, and edits index engagement, curation, or approval more than the effortful contribution the construct denotes. We therefore anchor the outcome on answers and report comment- and edit-inclusive alternatives in the decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}); upvotes, the one lower-cost channel we cannot observe at all, lie outside our processed data. As the decomposition shows, this choice does not manufacture the effect: comments alone carry no elevation, so broadening the outcome dilutes rather than inflates the estimate (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Treatment.} The treatment is the receipt of an answer. We operationalize this using \textit{treatment}, a binary variable indicating whether the user's question received at least one answer with a non-negative score. We adopt this operationalization rather than Stack Overflow's ``accepted answer'' mechanism to address endogeneity: marking an answer as accepted requires the user to return to the site, which may itself correlate with helping behavior.

\paragraph{Response Time.} For each treated observation, the response-time variable used in the interaction terms is $\log(1 + t_{\text{answer}})$, where $t_{\text{answer}}$ is the elapsed time in hours from the start of the observation window ($T_Q - 2\text{D}$) to the arrival of the first answer with a non-negative vote score. Because $T_Q - 2\text{D}$ is a fixed origin common to all observations, this is a monotone transformation of the actual response time in hours ($t_{\text{answer}} - t_{\text{question}}$) and preserves the full ordering of fast versus slow answers; however, it shifts the scale by the constant pre-question window length. For numerical stability, both response-time interaction terms ($\text{isTreatedActive} \times \log(1+t_{\text{answer}})$ and $\text{treatedPostQuestion} \times \log(1+t_{\text{answer}})$) are clipped to their 5th--95th percentile range and then standardized (z-scored) before model fitting. Consequently, the reported interaction coefficients $\gamma$ and $\delta$ are expressed per one standard deviation of the respective interaction term rather than per unit of $\log(1+t_{\text{answer}})$.

\paragraph{User Experience.} To test Hypothesis 2, we stratify our analysis by user tenure, defined as the number of days between a user's first activity on the platform and the start of the observation window. We define seven tenure buckets using right-closed intervals: $[0, 7]$, $(7, 30]$, $(30, 180]$, $(180, 365]$, $(365, 1095]$, $(1095, 2190]$, and $(2190, \infty)$ days, corresponding to the labels less than one week, one week to one month, one to six months, six to twelve months, one to three years, three to six years, and more than six years, respectively. Running separate models by bucket enables us to examine how the reciprocity effect varies across the user trajectory without imposing a parametric functional form.

\subsection{Propensity Score Matching}
\label{sec:matching}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Because the receipt of an answer (the treatment) is correlated with user characteristics (more experienced users, for instance, might tend to ask higher-quality questions that are more likely to receive answers), we employ propensity score matching \citep{rosenbaum_rubin_1983} to improve the comparability of the treatment and control groups. Importantly, matching is performed \textit{across different users}: each treated question (from a user who received an answer) is matched to a control question (from a different user whose question did not receive an answer) that has a similar propensity to receive an answer based on observable pre-treatment characteristics. This cross-user matching ensures that treated and control observations are independent conditional on the matched covariates, while the DiD structure within each pair absorbs residual differences.

We estimate the propensity score using pre-treatment covariates that jointly capture user experience, recent activity, and question characteristics, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:covariates}. The covariate \textit{tagAcceptShareAvg} addresses the concern that question complexity may confound the treatment: questions in tags with low accepted-answer rates are inherently more difficult, and matching on this rate ensures that treated and control questions come from similarly tractable topic areas.
Matching on the tag-level accepted-answer rate also matters for the response-time analysis, because it mitigates the concern that slower response times simply proxy for differently sized sub-communities with lower throughput. We use nearest-neighbor matching \emph{with replacement} and a caliper of 0.05 on the propensity score, requiring in addition an exact match on the calendar year, the question's primary tag, and the number of tags. Because the pool of unanswered questions is smaller than the pool of answered questions, some control questions are matched to more than one treated question; the matched file therefore contains more rows than unique question pairs (12.2 million pairs, 24.3 million rows). Each row enters the Cox analysis as an independent observation window; standard errors are discussed in the robustness appendix.

\subsection{Analytical Strategy: Cox Proportional Hazards with Time-Varying Covariates}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We model the hazard of helping using a Cox proportional hazards model with time-varying covariates. The counting-process formulation of \citet{andersen_gill_1982} extends the original Cox model \citep{cox_regression_1972} to settings with time-varying covariates and recurrent events, providing the large-sample theoretical foundation (including consistency and asymptotic normality of the partial likelihood estimator) that justifies our approach.

For each matched pair of observations (one treated, one control), we partition the 4-day observation window into intervals defined by the key event times: the start of the window, the posting of the question, and the arrival of the answer (the actual answer arrival for treated users or the synthetic transition for controls).

The hazard function is specified as:

\begin{multline}
\label{eq:cox}
h(t \mid \mathbf{X}(t)) = h_0(t) \exp\big(\beta_0 \cdot \text{treatment} + \beta_1 \cdot \text{phasePostQuestion}(t) + \beta_2 \cdot \text{treatedPostQuestion}(t) \\
+ \beta_3 \cdot \text{phasePostAnswer}(t) + \beta_4 \cdot \text{isTreatedActive}(t) \big)
\end{multline}

\noindent where $h_0(t)$ is the baseline hazard. \textit{treatment} is a time-constant indicator for treatment group membership (1 if the user's question received an answer, 0 otherwise); it absorbs stable between-group differences in baseline helping rates. The remaining covariates are time-varying indicators that switch on at the appropriate event times. Because \textit{treatedPostQuestion} switches on when the question is posted and remains on through the post-answer phase, the treated--control gap in the helping hazard equals $\beta_2$ during the waiting period and $\beta_2 + \beta_4$ after the answer arrives. The coefficient $\beta_4$ is therefore the difference-in-differences estimate of the change at answer arrival: the shift in the treated--control gap at the moment the answer lands, net of whatever divergence preceded it. We report $\beta_4$ as the treatment effect. The waiting-period coefficient $\beta_2$ is reported separately as a parallel-trends diagnostic: because no answer has yet arrived during the waiting period, it cannot reflect a response to answer receipt --- it measures anticipatory or selection-driven divergence, carries no sign guarantee, and is accordingly not folded into the effect estimate.

To test the moderation by response time (Hypothesis 3), we extend the model with two additional terms:

\begin{multline}
\label{eq:cox_speed}
h(t \mid \mathbf{X}(t)) = h_0(t) \exp\Big(\boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}(t) \\
+ \gamma \cdot \text{isTreatedActive}(t) \times \widetilde{\log}(\text{RT}) + \delta \cdot \text{treatedPostQuestion}(t) \times \widetilde{\log}(\text{RT}) \Big)
\end{multline}

\noindent where $\widetilde{\log}(\text{RT})$ denotes the standardized log response-time variable (see below), $\gamma$ captures the moderation of the answer-arrival increment by answer speed, and $\delta$ captures the analogous moderation during the waiting period. Just as the base treatment effect is the sum $\beta_2 + \beta_4$, the response-time moderation of that effect is the sum $\gamma + \delta$; we report this net moderation alongside its two components. A negative $\gamma + \delta$ would indicate that faster answers strengthen the reciprocity effect (consistent with H3), while a positive sum would indicate the opposite.

\paragraph{Stratification by Tenure.} To test Hypothesis 2, we fit separate models for each of the seven tenure buckets. This allows the baseline hazard and all coefficients to vary freely across levels of user experience, providing a nonparametric view of how reciprocity changes over the user trajectory.

\paragraph{Identification.} Our identification strategy rests on the matched DiD logic embedded in the model. Propensity score matching \citep{rosenbaum_rubin_1983} ensures that treated and control observations come from users with comparable baseline characteristics, including tenure, activity levels, and question topic difficulty. This matching absorbs between-user differences that might otherwise confound the treatment effect. We do not include user fixed effects in the survival models. Because matching is performed across different users (not within-user), and because the Cox model already leverages within-observation temporal variation through the time-varying covariate structure \citep{andersen_gill_1982}, user fixed effects are not required for identification. The DiD structure compares within-observation changes before and after the answer across treatment groups, isolating the treatment effect from stable individual differences. The waiting-period covariate (\textit{treatedPostQuestion}) captures any lift in helping that treated users show after asking but before receiving an answer. When this term is near zero, treated and control users move in parallel up to the answer, supporting the parallel-trends logic under which the arrival increment $\beta_4$ can be read as the effect of answer receipt rather than of the act of asking itself; when it is not, the two groups diverge before treatment arrives, and although $\beta_4$ nets out the average waiting-period gap, we flag such strata and interpret their estimates with added caution rather than treating the divergence as part of the effect. Even a near-zero waiting-period term, however, rules out only motivational shifts that materialize \textit{before} the answer: question-induced channels that operate mainly after the answer arrives --- most notably the elevated profile visibility a question generates --- are not absorbed by it (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). We therefore report the waiting-period term explicitly, so that departures from parallel trends are measured rather than assumed away, and we return to its magnitude in the Results.

\subsection{Generalized Reciprocity Effect (H1)}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------


To test Hypothesis~1, we estimate the pooled Cox model across all experience levels (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_experienced}). The treatment effect is the difference-in-differences increment at answer arrival ($\beta_4$); the waiting-period term ($\beta_2$) is reported alongside it as a parallel-trends diagnostic. The arrival increment is positive, statistically significant, and small: HR\,$=$\,1.03 (95\% CI [1.02, 1.05], $p<.001$), an approximately 3\% increase in the instantaneous rate of helping after the answer arrives. The waiting-period diagnostic is essentially zero ($\beta_2 =$\,[[AO:pooled-beta2]], n.s.), indicating that treated and control users moved in parallel until the answer arrived. Matched-pair bootstrap uncertainty for the pooled model, which preserves the matched structure, is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pair_bootstrap} and [[AO:boot-consistency --- confirms/qualifies]] the model-based interval. Hypothesis~1 therefore receives support in a deliberately modest form: the effect is far smaller than prior observational reports, and, as we show below, it is sensitive to conditioning on the quality of the answer received (Table~\ref{tab:answer_quality_robustness}).

A waiting-period term this close to zero indicates that the act of asking, by itself, did not shift treated users' helping during the wait; it does not rule out question-induced influences --- such as the increased profile visibility that a question generates --- that persist into the post-answer window (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). As we show next, the modest pooled effect masks substantial heterogeneity: the arrival increment is largest for newcomers and vanishes with tenure. We turn first to the decomposition of the outcome by help type.

\paragraph{Answer quality as an alternative explanation.} A natural concern, raised in review, is that response time proxies for answer quality: very fast answers are disproportionately terse or link-only, whereas answers that take longer to arrive tend to be fuller and more detailed, so it may be the quality of the answer --- not its timing --- that elicits reciprocal helping. We probe this directly by re-estimating the pooled speed specification with controls for three observable quality signals, re-extracted from the raw data dump for this revision: the length of the question's first answer in characters, its vote score, and whether it was subsequently accepted (Table~\ref{tab:answer_quality_robustness}). Entered one at a time, they tell a graded story. Controlling for \emph{answer length} alone --- the reviewer's terse-versus-detailed variable, and the signal least entangled with the asker's own behavior, since it is fixed by the answerer at composition --- [[AO:quality-length-effect --- verify: barely moves the estimate]] (arrival increment [[AO:quality-baseline-hr]] without controls, [[AO:quality-length-hr]] with length only). Controlling for the answer's vote score alone brings it to [[AO:quality-score-hr]], and the full specification, which additionally conditions on whether the answer was accepted, [[AO:quality-full-direction --- attenuates it further / pushes it below one]] (arrival [[AO:quality-full-hr]]), with the same pattern in the newcomer stratum (quality-controlled arrival [[AO:nc-quality-hr]]; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:newcomer_robustness}). The same three controls, applied within each discrete response-time bin rather than pooled, are reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:response_time_bins_quality} (discussed with the response-time results above). We read the fuller specification as a bounding exercise rather than a clean causal decomposition, because vote score and, especially, acceptance are post-treatment and endogenous to the outcome --- accepting requires the asker to return to the site, the same re-engagement that produces the helping outcome, so conditioning on it partials out part of the very channel through which reciprocity would operate. The upshot is two-sided. The association is robust to the reviewer's stated concern --- answer length --- and is reversed only by adjustment for the more endogenous signals, exactly as a bad-control logic anticipates; yet we cannot rule out that answer quality, imperfectly measured, is a genuine rival to any timing- or gratitude-based reading of the response-time pattern. The residual caveat is that character count is a coarse proxy for length: it does not distinguish worked code and explanation from boilerplate.
\input{analysis/output_tables/answer_quality_robustness.tex}

One interpretation of the response-time pattern draws on platform session dynamics: users who post a question may remain actively engaged while waiting, elevating helping during the waiting period regardless of whether an answer arrives. On this account, a fast answer might discharge that pending engagement rather than trigger graded reciprocal gratitude. This interpretation is speculative --- we do not observe session boundaries (logins, logouts, inactivity gaps). The answer-quality account above is an equally plausible reading of the same pattern, and a third, purely selective account is that users whose questions attract answers were simply more active to begin with. The design cannot adjudicate among these, and we present the session-dynamics reading as one candidate among several rather than as an established mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:help_rate_adoption_pooled} provides descriptive context, comparing users who have received an answer (red) with users who will receive one but are still waiting (blue). Figure~\ref{fig:help_rate_adoption_by_tenure} disaggregates this comparison by tenure bucket, and the disaggregation qualifies the pooled picture. In every panel beyond the newest users, the help rate of users still waiting exceeds that of users who have already received an answer, and both exceed the control baseline, up to approximately one to two hours post-question; only the newcomer panel ($<$\,1 week) shows the reverse ordering, with helping concentrated after the answer arrives. This is the disaggregated pattern the session-dynamics conjecture would predict --- a pending question temporarily elevates engagement, and answer receipt discharges it --- but, as noted, the design does not observe session boundaries, and answer quality offers a competing explanation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Methodological Contribution}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A persistent difficulty in studying generalized reciprocity from platform data is that the same user characteristics that predict seeking help also predict giving it. Active, engaged users are more likely both to post questions and to post answers, so correlations between help received and help given can reflect differences in user engagement rather than any causal response to receiving help. Prior observational studies that report positive associations between help-seeking and subsequent helping behavior without accounting for this selection \citep{baker_paying_2014, chen_engaging_2018, joyce_predicting_2006} may have overstated the role of reciprocity. The matched DiD survival design developed here addresses this confound by comparing changes in helping behavior, before and after question posting, between users whose questions received answers and propensity-score-matched users whose questions did not. Entering the waiting period as an explicit term makes any pre-answer divergence visible rather than silently absorbed into the treatment effect. In the present specification, the pooled contrast is small (arrival-increment HR\,$=$\,1.03) and the association steeply newcomer-concentrated --- substantially smaller than many earlier reports, and, as the answer-quality check shows, sensitive to controlling for the quality of the answer received, consistent with substantial confounding in prior designs.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reciprocity as a Contributor-Recruitment Mechanism}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The treatment effect shows a clear tenure gradient: the arrival increment is largest among the newest users (HR\,$=$\,[[AO:nc-arrival-hr]]) and falls to null among the most experienced (arrival HR\,$=$\,[[AO:b7-arrival-hr]]). We cannot directly observe whether this gradient reflects reciprocity, selection into being answered, the quality of the answers received, or the displacement of general moral motives by community-specific incentives --- the design identifies behavioral associations, not motivational shifts. If the gradient does reflect reciprocity rather than these alternatives, it would imply a narrow role for generalized reciprocity: an early association concentrated at the point of entry, before community-specific motivations are established.

As users accumulate experience, community-specific incentives may coincide with --- and plausibly displace --- the general reciprocal impulse, though we cannot directly observe this motivational shift. This is consistent with theories of norm displacement \citep{benabou_incentives_2006}, which show how the introduction of external incentives can crowd out moral motives. On Stack Overflow, reputation systems and status markers provide salient, community-specific reasons to help that are not available to newcomers. The near-null association among established users [[AO:b7-reversal --- if the >6-year arrival HR is significantly below one, add: --- and, among those with more than six years, a small but statistically significant reversal (arrival HR\,$=$\,X.XX, $p<.05$) ---]] is consistent with this account, though it is equally consistent with selection into being answered in that stratum.

This reframing also helps account for the inconsistency of findings in the prior literature. Studies that draw primarily on samples of experienced users, or that aggregate across experience levels dominated by experienced contributors, should find weaker reciprocity effects under this account. Studies that capture behavior early in the user trajectory should find stronger ones. The variation in prior findings may thus reflect not noise or methodological failure, but genuine heterogeneity in the effect across the user population.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Response-Time Pattern and Its Interpretation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The response-time analysis was intended to test the prediction, derived from laboratory studies of gratitude, that faster help produces stronger reciprocity. The pattern offers it qualified support. The continuous interaction runs in the predicted direction --- the arrival increment is larger for faster answers in every tenure bucket --- and the discrete bins locate the effect in an early response-time window, with a pronounced peak for answers arriving within thirty to sixty minutes. This is broadly consistent with an emotional-immediacy account, in which help that arrives while the need is still salient elicits a stronger reciprocal response. Two features counsel caution. The very fastest answers (within thirty minutes) show no elevation, which a pure immediacy account does not predict; and the same specification's base effect [[AO:quality-full-direction-discussion --- attenuates markedly / falls below one]] once answer quality is controlled, so we cannot rule out that the timing pattern partly reflects the quality of fast versus slow answers rather than their speed per se.

Two non-gratitude readings could also generate an early-window peak. The first is answer quality: answers that arrive within the first few minutes may be terse or link-only, while those in the 30--60-minute window may be substantive enough to prompt reciprocation; controlling for observable quality does [[AO:quality-channel-discussion --- move the base effect toward/below one]], consistent with this channel. The second is platform session dynamics: an answer that arrives while a user is still in an active session may catch them at a moment of elevated engagement, whereas an answer that arrives after the session has ended cannot. We advance both only as conjectures; our data cannot cleanly distinguish them from a genuine immediacy effect or from selection, because we observe neither answer text nor the session boundaries (logins, logouts, inactivity gaps) that a direct test would require. Conditioning the response-time bins on answer quality (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:response_time_bins_quality}) [[AO:bin-3060-quality-discussion --- verify: leaves the 30--60-minute peak intact --- it attenuates but remains the clear early maximum (X.XX to X.XX) ---]] so the early-window advantage is not an artifact of fast answers being better answers. [[AO:late-bump-discussion --- if present:]] A weaker secondary elevation at multi-day delays also appears, but because the quality controls are post-treatment we cannot attach a clean mechanism to it and do not build on it. The immediacy pattern is thus supported for the early window, subject to the usual caution about observational, quality-adjusted comparisons.

The response-time pattern bears only weakly on the norm-displacement account. The arrival increment and its response-time moderation are both near zero among the most experienced users, which is at least consistent with the mechanism as a whole having faded rather than merely dampened. We do not lean on the response-time pattern as evidence for displacement.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implications for Platform Design}
\label{sec:design_implications}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Because any reciprocity-like elevation is modest and concentrated among newcomers, the practical leverage platforms can derive from it lies primarily in contributor recruitment rather than in sustaining contributions from established members --- and even that recruitment effect is small in absolute terms.

The most direct implication is that platforms seeking to grow their contributor base should prioritize ensuring that new users receive answers to their questions. In absolute terms, the newcomer arrival increment (HR\,$=$\,[[AO:nc-arrival-hr]]; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}) translates to an additional helping-event risk of roughly [[AO:nc-ard-pp]] percentage points per observation window, with a number-needed-to-treat of about [[AO:nc-nnt]]: roughly [[AO:nc-nnt]] newcomer questions must receive an answer to yield one additional helping event to others in the window (Table~\ref{tab:absolute_effects}). The pooled effect across all tenure levels is far smaller (number-needed-to-treat near [[AO:pooled-nnt]]) and sensitive to answer-quality controls, so the design implication is not a platform-wide helping multiplier but a targeted --- and modest --- newcomer onboarding channel.

Our results provide only tentative support for a response-time lever. The reciprocity signal is concentrated in answers that arrive early, with a peak in the 30--60-minute window (Tables~\ref{tab:speed_results} and~\ref{tab:response_time_bins}), which is consistent with prioritizing prompt answers to newcomers. But because the effect is small and we cannot fully separate answer speed from answer quality --- even though the 30--60-minute advantage does survive quality controls --- we stop short of recommending interventions built specifically on response speed. The better-supported emphasis is answer receipt itself --- ensuring that newcomers' questions are answered at all, and promptly, and that answer notifications are salient --- rather than optimizing for a particular response-time window.

A further implication concerns the increasing prevalence of AI-generated answers on question-and-answer platforms (see also the GAI-era limitation below). If AI assistance substitutes for human responses, the human-to-human interaction that could sustain generalized reciprocity may decline. Given that any residual association is concentrated among newcomers, this substitution could weaken an early onboarding channel through which new users currently receive a modest push into helping from human community members.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Several limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn from this analysis.

The most important identification caveat is right-truncation and churn: users who post a question and leave the platform before the fixed post-question window ends are under-observed for the outcome in ways correlated with both treatment and helping. We do not implement a Heckman-type correction; instead, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:selection_bounds} reports transparent bounds on the contrast under alternative assumptions about zero-event controls. Even under optimistic bounds, the pooled effect remains modest; the newcomer contrast is more sensitive to the churn assumption and should be read with that sensitivity in mind.

Our helping outcome is the posting of an answer to another user's question (self-directed accepts excluded); the decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:outcome_decomposition}) shows this choice is not what generates the effect --- comments alone carry no elevation, and adding them dilutes the estimate. Two further low-cost reciprocal channels that novices may favor nonetheless sit outside the outcome: edits of others' posts, which we observe and report separately (edits-only arrival HR 1.14) but keep out of the headline outcome because they index curation more than costly contribution, and upvotes, which are absent from our processed data and would require re-parsing the raw dump to recover. To the extent that newcomers reciprocate disproportionately through these unmeasured or separately tabulated channels, our answers-anchored estimates do not capture that portion of reciprocal behavior, and the reported hazard ratios describe costly contribution specifically rather than a complete accounting of reciprocity. We also cannot cleanly separate a reciprocal response to receiving help from selection into being answered.

Beyond identification, the design captures a behavioral association but cannot identify the psychological mechanism producing it. Gratitude, moral obligation, incentive displacement, and platform-session dynamics are all \textit{proposed} interpretations consistent with parts of the pattern; the data do not allow us to distinguish among them, and we do not observe session boundaries, reputation salience, or emotional states. A particularly consequential competing explanation is answer quality. Faster answers are systematically terser than slower ones; while the pooled association is [[AO:quality-length-limitation --- verify: robust to controlling for answer length alone]], adding the more endogenous quality signals --- vote score and, especially, whether the first answer was accepted --- [[AO:quality-full-limitation --- attenuates the pooled base effect markedly / pushes it below one]] (Table~\ref{tab:answer_quality_robustness}), so we cannot attribute the association to answer receipt net of answer quality. These controls are imperfect: acceptance and score are post-treatment and partly endogenous (accepting requires the asker to return to the site), and character count captures answer length only coarsely, without distinguishing worked code and explanation from filler. The direction is nonetheless a genuine caveat, and it counts against a quality-free reading of the effect. Future work combining behavioral records with experience-sampling measures of emotional state or moral reasoning during platform use could help disentangle these pathways.

The identification strategy rests on the assumption that, conditional on the matched covariates and the DiD structure, answer receipt is approximately independent of the potential outcomes for helping behavior. The propensity score matching substantially improves the comparability of treated and control observations, as confirmed by post-matching balance diagnostics. Unobserved time-varying factors, including changes in workload, access to alternative information sources, or exogenous variation in a user's availability, could nonetheless bias the estimates in ways that the design cannot rule out.

A specific unobserved channel of this kind is exposure. Posting a question raises the asker's profile visibility: other users who encounter the question may view the asker's profile and contribution history, and this salience can generate reputation concern or social-obligation pressure that is distinct from gratitude for a received answer yet persists into the post-answer window. The waiting-period covariate (\textit{treatedPostQuestion}) absorbs the baseline effect of the act of asking, but not any residual visibility that continues after the answer arrives, so the design cannot fully separate gratitude for answer receipt from this exposure channel. The threat is to mechanism attribution rather than to the existence of the behavioral pattern: the arrival increment remains a well-defined estimate of the behavioral change at answer receipt, but part of any elevation could reflect exposure rather than reciprocity. A natural probe --- a placebo test asking whether unanswered questions with \textit{high} view counts show a helping lift in a pseudo-post-answer window --- is unfortunately not identified with the archival data available to us: the Stack Overflow data dump records \textit{ViewCount} only as a single cumulative snapshot at the dump date, so a question's view count conflates its age, topic popularity, and years of subsequent (largely search-engine) traffic with the contemporaneous exposure during the days-long observation window that such a placebo would require. Testing the exposure channel directly would require time-stamped view data or repeated dump snapshots that do not exist for our study period.

Response time may partially proxy for question characteristics not fully captured by the tag-level accepted-answer rate, since questions that attract fast responses may differ systematically from those that wait. Future work with random treatment or finer-grained measures of question difficulty, such as text-based complexity or specificity scores, could provide a cleaner test of the response-time mechanism.

The session-dynamics and re-engagement interpretations rest on indirect evidence: discrete-bin patterns, continuous speed interactions, and descriptive help-rate figures. Direct tests would require session-level data, including login and logout timestamps or inactivity-based session boundaries. We treat these accounts as speculative conjectures, not established mechanisms.

Stack Overflow is a large, relatively anonymous, gamified platform built around technical question-answering. The patterns we estimate may not transfer to smaller or relation-based communities (enterprise Slack channels, academic peer review, intimate hobby forums) where social ties and non-status motives differ. We therefore refrain from claiming that generalized reciprocity ``likely operates'' elsewhere; at most, our estimates describe one platform's pre-/early-generative-AI era.

The analysis window spans Stack Overflow's inception through 1 April 2025 and should be read as a \textit{historical snapshot}, not a prescriptive guide for contemporary platform design. Since 2023, LLM-based tools have structurally disrupted Stack Overflow's ecosystem: active question volume and human-to-human answering have declined sharply. The human-answer reciprocity associations we estimate were formed when community answering was the norm; their relevance to platforms increasingly mediated by AI-generated responses is uncertain. The pattern is not, however, confined to the recent AI-affected period: restricting the sample to the pre-2020 era leaves both the pooled association and the newcomer association essentially unchanged (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:cohort_robustness}), so what we document characterizes the pre-generative-AI platform rather than a transient artifact of its disruption.

Finally, the analysis establishes that any short-run association declines with experience but does not characterize what replaces it. The theoretical argument implicates community-specific norms and status motives, but testing this account would require integrating behavioral analysis with direct measures of those alternative mechanisms, for instance, through surveys or experimental manipulations of reputation visibility.

%=============================================================
\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion} %=============================================================
Generalized reciprocity is widely theorized as a mechanism sustaining cooperation on knowledge-sharing platforms, but observational evidence is limited by a persistent confound: active users are more likely both to seek help and to give it, regardless of whether their questions are resolved. Our matched difference-in-differences survival design confronts this confound directly. The pooled treatment effect is positive but small (difference-in-differences arrival increment HR\,$=$\,1.03), steeply concentrated among newcomers (arrival HR\,$=$\,[[AO:nc-arrival-hr]]) and undetectable among veterans. The newcomer association is the most robust pattern in the data --- it holds in the pre-2020, pre-generative-AI subsample as well as in the full sample (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:cohort_robustness}) --- yet even it is modest in absolute terms and [[AO:quality-conclusion --- is sensitive to / does not survive]] controls for the quality of the answer received. Receiving help appears to nudge newcomers toward helping others, but our estimates cannot, on their own, separate that story from selection into being answered or from the quality of the help that arrives; distinguishing them remains a task for designs with exogenous variation in whether, and how quickly, help arrives.
